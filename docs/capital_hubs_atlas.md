## TL;DR
Over ~6,000 years the world's dominant "capital hub" migrated from the irrigated river valleys of Mesopotamia and Egypt, through the commercial Mediterranean (Phoenicia, Athens, Rome), eastward to the true economic giants of Asia (Abbasid Baghdad, Song China, Mughal India — which together held the majority of world GDP for most of recorded history), then decisively westward in the "Great Divergence" to Atlantic Europe (Lisbon, Seville, Amsterdam, London) and finally to the United States (New York, Silicon Valley), with East Asia now re-rising — and at every stage wealth was built through some mix of three mechanisms: EXTRACTION (mining/farming), COMMERCE (trade/finance/manufacturing), and WAR-TRANSFER (conquest/plunder/tribute).

The single most important long-run fact, established by Angus Maddison's historical GDP estimates, is that Asia — not Europe — was the economic center of the world for most of history: India and China alone produced something like half of world output as late as 1700, and China's share peaked at an estimated 32.9% of world GDP in 1820 (per the U.S. Congressional Research Service, "In 1820, China accounted for an estimated 33% of global GDP on a PPP basis," falling to 5% by 1978) before collapsing further; Europe's dominance is a recent, ~250-year aberration now partially unwinding.

The relationship between capital and political power evolved from total fusion (god-kings and temple-states who WERE the wealth) toward progressive separation and then inversion: by the era of the Medici, the VOC, the Rothschilds, the Bank of England and modern Wall Street/Silicon Valley, an autonomous financial class increasingly dictated political and military outcomes rather than merely serving rulers — money began to follow money, and power increasingly followed money.

## Key Findings
- **Three mechanisms, recurring in sequence.** Nodes that rose on raw EXTRACTION (Egypt's Nubian gold, Athens' Laurion silver, Potosí) tended to be fragile when the ore or soil gave out; nodes built on COMMERCE and especially FINANCIAL INNOVATION (Amsterdam, London, New York) proved the most durable and compounding; WAR-TRANSFER (Assyria, Rome, the Mongols, colonial Europe) produced spectacular but often one-time windfalls that had to be converted into commerce or extraction to last.
- **The "East-to-West" story is really "East-then-West-then-East-again."** Asia's multi-millennial dominance, the medieval Islamic golden age, the brief Western interlude (c. 1500–2000) driven by New World silver + Atlantic slavery + coal/industrialization, and the 21st-century re-rise of China and East Asia form a single arc.
- **Financial technology is the great accelerator.** Coinage (Lydia/Athens/Persia), paper money (Song China), bills of exchange and double-entry bookkeeping (medieval Italy), the joint-stock company and stock exchange (Amsterdam 1602), central banking and sovereign debt (Bank of England 1694), and venture capital (Silicon Valley) each let a node punch far above its raw-resource weight.
- **Capital's relationship to power flips over time.** From temple-states and god-kings (wealth = ruler) to merchant republics and banking dynasties (wealth distinct from, then leveraging, ruler) to the modern era where financial markets discipline states.

## Details

### I. The First Cities and Temple Economies — Sumer/Mesopotamia (c. 4000–2000 BCE)
The first true capital hubs were the city-states of southern Mesopotamia, above all Uruk (c. 4000–3100 BCE), which by ~3000 BCE was a walled city of over two square miles housing perhaps tens of thousands of people — likely the largest settlement humanity had yet produced. Dominant mechanism: EXTRACTION (irrigation agriculture); secondary: COMMERCE. The wealth base was the grain surplus wrung from the Tigris–Euphrates floodplain by intensive canal irrigation, whose maintenance was directed by the great temple estates. That agricultural surplus freed labor for pottery, textile manufacture, and metalworking, and funded long-distance trade for the timber, stone, and metals the alluvial plain lacked. Later Sumerian and Akkadian centers — Ur, Lagash, Akkad — added a WAR-TRANSFER layer as kings conquered neighbors.

Capital–power relationship: total fusion. This was the purest form of the temple-state: each city was literally the property of its god, administered by the priesthood (ensi) and later the king (lugal); the temple was simultaneously the warehouse of agricultural surplus, the redistribution center, and the seat of power. The wealth-holders and the rulers were the same institution. There was no separate merchant class controlling capital — the "1%" were the gods and their earthly managers.

### II. Pharaonic Egypt — Nile Surplus and Nubian Gold (c. 3100–1070 BCE)
Egypt's wealth, concentrated at Memphis and later Thebes, rested on two pillars. Dominant: EXTRACTION (Nile agriculture + gold mining); secondary: WAR-TRANSFER (tribute from Nubia and the Levant). The predictable Nile flood produced reliable grain surpluses; the Eastern Desert and Nubia held an estimated ~250 gold-production sites (the Wadi Hammamat region a key quarry/mining zone, famously mapped on the Ramesside-era Turin Papyrus Map c. 1160 BCE). Gold was so abundant in the New Kingdom (c. 1550–1070 BCE) that it was valued at only about one-third the price of silver; tellingly, after Egypt lost control of Nubia, gold's relative value roughly tripled by c. 960 BCE — direct evidence that Egyptian prosperity hinged on Nubian extraction.

Capital–power relationship: fusion (god-king). The pharaoh was a living god who, in theory, owned the land, the mines, and the harvest. Mining was state-organized; tribute from conquered Nubia and Canaan flowed directly to the palace and temples (depicted in reliefs such as the tomb of Huy, viceroy of Kush under Tutankhamun). A command economy with no autonomous capitalists.

### III. Bronze Age Trade Systems and the Phoenician Commercial Revolution (c. 2000–500 BCE)
The Bronze Age ran on a metals-trade network — copper (Cyprus) and tin (as far as Afghanistan and possibly Cornwall) fused into bronze — whose interdependence helped make the system vulnerable to the Bronze Age Collapse (c. 1200–1150 BCE). Out of that collapse rose the first civilization built primarily on COMMERCE: the Phoenician city-states of Tyre, Sidon, and Byblos (c. 1200–600 BCE), and their great western colony Carthage (founded c. 814 BCE). Dominant: COMMERCE; secondary: EXTRACTION (manufacturing of dye and glass). The Phoenicians monopolized Tyrian purple dye (extracted laboriously from murex shells — a luxury good literally worth more than its weight in silver), exported Lebanese cedar, glass, and textiles, and acted as middlemen funneling Iberian silver and tin, African gold and ivory across the Mediterranean. Tyre supplied the materials for Solomon's Temple; Carthage inherited the trade and the navy.

Capital–power relationship: the first merchant oligarchies. Carthage was governed by a council of wealthy merchants and aristocrats who made decisions on commercial-pragmatic grounds. Here capital and political power begin to separate from sacral kingship: the wealth-holders were a commercial elite, not god-kings, though they still controlled the state directly.

### IV. Tribute Empires — Assyria, Babylon, and Achaemenid Persia (c. 900–330 BCE)
The Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian empires were the archetypes of WAR-TRANSFER: wealth concentrated at Nineveh and Babylon through systematic conquest, deportation, plunder, and tribute. The Achaemenid Persian Empire (c. 550–330 BCE) under Darius I rationalized this into history's first great administrative tribute machine. Dominant: WAR-TRANSFER (tribute); secondary: COMMERCE (coinage + Royal Road). According to Herodotus, Darius organized roughly 20 satrapies paying a total of about 14,560 silver talents annually (Babylon ~1,000 talents plus grain; Egypt ~700 talents plus 120,000 measures of grain; India ~360 talents of gold dust). Darius introduced a bimetallic coinage — the gold daric and silver siglos — borrowed from conquered Lydia (the world's first coiners), and built the ~2,500 km Royal Road from Susa to Sardis, crossed by royal couriers in about 15 days, which simultaneously served military control and commerce.

Capital–power relationship: fusion via a service aristocracy. Wealth concentrated in the king and a Persian noble class who governed as satraps; much tribute arrived as raw bullion to be minted. Merchants benefited from the infrastructure but did not control it.

### V. Classical Athens — Silver, Trade, and the Empire of the Owl (5th c. BCE)
Athens is the cleanest case of a node pivoting through all three mechanisms in a single century. Dominant: EXTRACTION (Laurion silver) → COMMERCE (maritime trade) → WAR-TRANSFER (the Delian League tribute). The silver mines of Laurion, worked by tens of thousands of enslaved laborers, were the foundation: in 483/2 BCE a rich new vein yielded about 100 talents (~2.6 tonnes) of silver, and on Themistocles' advice Athens spent the windfall building ~200 triremes — the fleet that won Salamis in 480 BCE. Peak fifth-century output reached roughly 20,000 kg (about 31 tonnes) of silver a year, minted into the famous "owl" tetradrachms that became the Mediterranean's reserve currency, lubricating Athenian trade. Victory over Persia then let Athens convert the defensive Delian League (478/7 BCE) into a tributary empire, appropriating allied contributions to rebuild the city (the Parthenon) and fund the navy.

Capital–power relationship: democratic state as the capitalist. The mines were public property leased to private entrepreneurs (who rented slave gangs); the silver and the tribute flowed to the democratic state, which deployed it for fleet and monuments. Wealth and political power were broadly held by the citizen body — but rested on slavery and imperial extraction.

### VI. Hellenistic Alexandria and the Roman Colossus (c. 330 BCE – 200 CE)
Alexandria (founded 331 BCE) became the Hellenistic world's great COMMERCE-and-grain entrepôt and intellectual capital (the Museum and Library). Then Rome absorbed the Mediterranean into a single political-economic system. Rome's dominant mechanism shifted over time: WAR-TRANSFER (Republic) → EXTRACTION + COMMERCE (Empire). The Republic's expansion was a self-financing engine of plunder, indemnities, tribute, and mass enslavement — victorious generals returned with gold, silver, and slaves that enriched the elite and funded further conquest. Conquered Spain delivered the great prize: the silver and copper mines of Baetica and the Rio Tinto district, where precious-metal mines became state property while base-metal mines stayed partly private. Under the Empire, the system matured into provincial taxation (Augustus largely abolished the predatory publicani tax-farmers, levying instead a ~1% wealth tax and a poll tax), Egyptian and African grain to feed Rome's million inhabitants, and vast manufacturing and trade. A notable counter-flow: Rome ran a chronic luxury-trade deficit with India and China — Pliny the Elder famously (and probably with moralizing exaggeration) claimed Rome lost ~100 million sesterces a year to India, China, and Arabia for silks, spices, and pearls.

Capital–power relationship: conquest creates an oligarchy that captures the state. Plunder enriched senatorial families whose wealth then bought political power, destabilizing the Republic; under the Empire, mineral wealth became "the prize of usurpation," and the emperor's fiscal apparatus fused capital and sovereign power.

### VII. The Parallel Asian Pole — Han and Tang China and the Silk Road (c. 200 BCE – 907 CE)
While Rome dominated the Mediterranean, Han China (206 BCE–220 CE) anchored the eastern end of Eurasia. Dominant: EXTRACTION (vast agrarian base + state salt/iron monopolies); secondary: COMMERCE (silk exports). Chinese silk traveled the Silk Road westward through Parthian and Indian middlemen (the two empires never traded directly — the Han envoy Gan Ying turned back in 97 CE), and it was Chinese silk that helped drain Roman silver eastward. Domestically, silk itself served as a money — taxes and salaries were paid in bolts of cloth. Tang China (618–907) and its capital Chang'an (modern Xi'an) marked a commercial high point: Chang'an was almost certainly the largest city on earth, with on the order of a million people inside the walls and a cosmopolitan foreign quarter, the eastern terminus of the Silk Road. The true economic artery was the Grand Canal (completed 609 under the Sui), which by 735 CE moved enormous quantities of tax grain north each year.

Capital–power relationship: bureaucratic-imperial control. China's distinctive pattern: a powerful Confucian state bureaucracy regulated markets, guilds, prices, and monopolies, keeping merchants socially subordinate and politically dependent — the opposite of the autonomous merchant republics of the West.

### VIII. The Islamic Golden Age — Abbasid Baghdad and the Cairo Trade (c. 750–1258)
After the Arab conquests, the economic and intellectual center of the Western world moved to the Abbasid Caliphate and its capital Baghdad, founded 762 by al-Mansur, which by the reign of Harun al-Rashid (786–809) had become the richest and possibly largest city on earth, with a population approaching a million. Dominant: COMMERCE (control of east-west trade); secondary: EXTRACTION (agriculture + precious metals) and scholarship. Baghdad sat astride both the Silk Road and the Indian Ocean "sea roads," its bazaars connecting Scandinavia to China; the gold dinar and silver dirham were the international reserve currencies of the age (Abbasid dinars have been found as far as England and Scandinavia). This wealth funded the House of Wisdom, the great translation-and-research academy. As the Abbasid center fragmented after the 10th century, the commercial baton passed to Fatimid and Mamluk Cairo, which controlled the lucrative Red Sea spice route into the Mediterranean, and to Córdoba in Islamic Spain.

Capital–power relationship: a wealthy state plus a thriving merchant class. The caliph's court was the great wealth-concentrator, but Islamic commercial law, partnerships, and credit instruments supported a genuinely autonomous merchant class operating across the whole network.

### IX. The Wealthiest Place on Earth — Song China (960–1279)
By many measures the high-water mark of pre-modern economic sophistication anywhere on the planet was Song China, centered on Kaifeng (Northern Song) and then Hangzhou (Southern Song). Dominant: COMMERCE + proto-industry; secondary: EXTRACTION (agriculture, coal, iron). The Song experienced what historians call a "commercial revolution": the world's first government-issued paper money (the jiaozi, originating in Sichuan), fast-ripening Champa rice enabling double-cropping and a population that reached ~100 million, and a proto-industrial iron-and-coal complex. By Robert Hartwell's much-cited (and by Donald Wagner contested) estimate, China produced about 125,000 tonnes of iron annually by 1078 — a level Europe would not match until the 18th century — using coke-fired blast furnaces and water-powered bellows. Many historians argue Song China came closer to an industrial revolution than anywhere before 18th-century Britain.

Capital–power relationship: bureaucratic state harnessing markets. The state monopolized currency issuance and key industries (salt, parts of iron and silk) while letting competitive private industry flourish elsewhere — but no autonomous capitalist class ever escaped bureaucratic supervision, which many scholars cite as a reason the proto-industrial surge did not become self-sustaining.

### X. The Mongol Eurasian Bridge — Pax Mongolica (1206–1368)
The Mongol Empire, the largest contiguous land empire in history, enriched itself first through stupendous WAR-TRANSFER — conquest, plunder, and tribute on a continental scale — and then deliberately fostered COMMERCE. Dominant: WAR-TRANSFER; secondary: COMMERCE. Under the Pax Mongolica (c. 1250–1350), the Mongols protected the Yam postal relay system, financed merchant caravans through state-backed ortoq partnerships, and circulated paper money, unifying the overland Silk Road under a single security umbrella and dramatically lowering the cost and risk of trans-Eurasian trade (the world of Marco Polo and Ibn Battuta). Findlay and O'Rourke argue that meaningful globalization "began with the unification of the central Eurasian landmass by the Mongol conquests." The same integrated trade routes then carried the Black Death west in the 1340s, helping unravel the system.

Capital–power relationship: military aristocracy plus sponsored merchants. The khans and their warrior elite were the wealth-holders; merchants prospered as state-licensed agents (ortoq), a fusion of conquest-power and state-backed commerce.

### XI. The Medieval European Revival — Italian City-States, the Champagne Fairs, and the Hanseatic League (c. 1100–1450)
Western Europe re-entered the first rank through COMMERCE. The Italian maritime republics — Venice, Genoa, Pisa — grew rich on the Levant trade, hugely boosted by the Crusades. Venice secured duty-free trading rights across the Byzantine Empire via the Golden Bull of 1082, and in 1204 the Fourth Crusade — financed and redirected by the Venetians under Doge Enrico Dandolo — sacked Constantinople itself, a brazen WAR-TRANSFER that enriched Venice with Byzantine territory and plunder. (Constantinople itself had been the West's premier COMMERCE entrepôt for centuries, its gold solidus/bezant holding a stable value for some 700 years and its silk a state monopoly, before Italian merchants progressively captured its trade.) The Champagne Fairs of France (12th–13th c.) — a staggered cycle across Troyes, Provins, Lagny, and Bar-sur-Aube — linked Flemish cloth with Italian/Levantine luxuries and became the cradle of the bill of exchange; the Hanseatic League, led by Lübeck, dominated the bulk trade of the Baltic and North Sea (grain, salted herring preserved with Lüneburg salt, furs, timber, cloth) through its network of Kontore at Bruges, London, Bergen, and Novgorod, winning a near-monopoly via the Treaty of Stralsund (1370).

Capital–power relationship: the merchant republic. Here capital openly became sovereign: Venice, Genoa, and the Hanseatic cities were polities literally governed by and for their commercial elites, with their own navies and foreign policies. Money now WAS the state.

### XII. West African Gold — Mali, Timbuktu, and Mansa Musa (13th–15th c.)
The Mali Empire, controlling the West African goldfields and the southern terminus of the trans-Saharan trade, was for a time one of the richest states on earth. Dominant: EXTRACTION (gold) + COMMERCE (controlling/taxing trade routes); secondary: EXTRACTION (Niger-valley agriculture). Caravans carried gold, salt, ivory, and enslaved people across the Sahara; cities like Timbuktu, Gao, and Djenné became hubs of commerce and Islamic scholarship. The legendary 1324 pilgrimage of Mansa Musa — often called the wealthiest individual in history — reportedly distributed so much gold in Cairo that it depressed the metal's value there for years, and put Mali on European maps (the 1375 Catalan Atlas depicts Musa holding a gold nugget), helping inspire later Portuguese efforts to reach the source of the gold directly.

Capital–power relationship: the emperor as gold-monopolist. The mansa taxed the trade and was identified with the gold itself; wealth and sacral-political authority were fused, though an active merchant and scholarly class thrived in the cities. (Parallel African nodes: Aksum, which struck sub-Saharan Africa's first gold coinage c. 270 CE on the Red Sea trade; Great Zimbabwe, a gold-and-cattle tribute state; and the Swahili Coast city-states like Kilwa, Indian Ocean entrepôts brokering African gold for Asian goods.)

### XIII. Renaissance Florence — Banking and the Medici (14th–15th c.)
While Venice traded, Florence banked and manufactured. Dominant: COMMERCE (banking + textiles); secondary: EXTRACTION (alum monopoly). Florentine wealth rested on the wool and silk textile industries and on finance. The Medici Bank, founded in 1397 by Giovanni di Bicci de' Medici, deployed and popularized double-entry bookkeeping, bills of exchange, and a holding-company structure of semi-autonomous branch partnerships across Rome, Venice, London, Bruges, and beyond. As papal bankers and (after a grant from Pope Pius II) holders of a near-monopoly on the alum mines of Tolfa (alum being essential to textile dyeing), the Medici became Europe's most powerful financial house.

Capital–power relationship: the banking dynasty BECOMES the state. The Medici are the pivotal case in the whole timeline: a private banking family that used financial wealth to take over a republic, produce popes and queens, and bankroll the Renaissance. Here financial capital begins, unmistakably, to purchase and dictate political power.

### XIV. Mughal India — The Manufacturing Superpower (c. 1526–1750)
For most of this period the largest economy on earth was not in Europe at all but in South Asia. Mughal India's dominant mechanism: EXTRACTION (vast agrarian tax base + cash crops) + COMMERCE (textile manufacturing/export). By Maddison-derived estimates, India accounted for roughly 24–25% of world GDP around 1700; and per economic historian Paul Bairoch ("International Industrialization Levels from 1750 to 1980," Journal of European Economic History, 1982), India accounted for ~24.5% of world manufacturing output in 1750 — as Clingingsmith & Williamson (NBER w10586) summarize, "While India produced about 25 percent of world industrial output in 1750, this figure fell to only 2 percent by 1900." The anchor industry was textiles: India was the world's dominant producer of cotton cloth, and Bengal (around Dhaka) was its workshop, producing fine muslins and calicos consumed from Japan to the Americas; Bengal alone contributed roughly 12% of the empire's GDP. Demand for Indian textiles was so one-sided that Europeans had little to offer in exchange but bullion — they shipped American silver to India to pay for cloth. In the late 17th–early 18th century Mughal India accounted for around 95% of British imports from Asia.

Capital–power relationship: imperial land-revenue state. Mughal wealth was rooted in a sophisticated land-tax system (Akbar's treasury revenues were enormous); the emperor and the mansabdar nobility were the great wealth-holders, with merchants and financier-bankers (like the Jagat Seths of Bengal) powerful but subordinate — until the English East India Company turned military force into a new extraction engine after the Battle of Plassey (1757) and Buxar (1764).

### XV. The Atlantic Pivot — Portugal/Lisbon and Spain/Seville (c. 1450–1650)
The decisive geographic shift of capital toward the Atlantic began with the Iberian sea empires. Portugal/Lisbon's dominant mechanism: COMMERCE (monopoly maritime spice trade) + EXTRACTION (West African gold). After Vasco da Gama reached India (1498), the state-run Casa da Índia organized the annual India Armadas and enforced a royal pepper monopoly so lucrative that Manuel I was nicknamed "the grocer king"; by the late 16th century spices supplied roughly half of Portuguese government revenue.

Spain/Seville's dominant mechanism: EXTRACTION (New World silver) channeled through a state COMMERCE monopoly; secondary: WAR-TRANSFER (conquest + the encomienda/mita forced-labor systems). The Casa de Contratación (Seville, 1503) monopolized New World trade and ran the treasure fleets, taxing bullion at the 20% quinto real. The mountain of Potosí (Cerro Rico, discovered 1545, in modern Bolivia) plus the Mexican mines made Spanish America the source of perhaps 80% of the world's silver; estimates suggest Spanish America produced on the order of 150,000 tonnes of silver between 1500 and 1800, worked by the brutal mita forced-labor draft (Potosí became, around 1600, one of the largest cities in the world). Crucially, much of this silver did not stay in Europe: via the Manila Galleon (Acapulco–Manila, from 1565), a large share flowed onward to China, which was monetizing its economy on silver — Flynn and Giráldez date the "birth of world trade" to 1571 precisely because of this silver loop linking Potosí, Seville, Manila, and the Ming economy.

Capital–power relationship: the crown as chief merchant — and the failure of pure extraction. Both empires ran their wealth through royal monopolies. But Spain is the cautionary tale of extraction without productive reinvestment: the silver largely flowed out again to service Habsburg war debts and pay mercenary armies, fueling inflation (the "Price Revolution") and ultimately enriching Spain's commercial rivals (Dutch, English, and Chinese) more than Spain itself.

### XVI. The First Modern Financial Center — Amsterdam and the Dutch Golden Age (c. 1600–1700)
The Dutch Republic turned commerce into modern capitalism. Dominant: COMMERCE (trade + financial innovation); secondary: WAR-TRANSFER (colonial monopoly violence). Amsterdam's foundation was actually the bulk Baltic grain trade, but its world-historical innovations were financial: the Dutch East India Company (VOC), chartered 1602, was the first company to make tradable shares available to the general public, creating the Amsterdam Stock Exchange; the Bank of Amsterdam (1609) was a precursor of the central bank. The VOC raised 6,440,200 guilders in its August 1602 IPO across six chambers (Amsterdam providing 57%) — when the IPO closed on August 31, 1602, 1,143 investors had subscribed to the initial capital of the company's Amsterdam branch. The VOC held a state-granted Asian trade monopoly with quasi-sovereign powers (it could wage war, mint coins, and found colonies), and its shares spawned the first true secondary market, complete with futures, options, and short-selling. By the later 17th century Amsterdam was the world's first modern financial center; as Dutch commercial supremacy faded, the Republic reinvented itself as the financier of Europe, placing the government loans of foreign monarchs.

Capital–power relationship: capital fully sovereign, and outsourced violence. The VOC is the archetype of capital wielding state power directly — the Dutch government literally outsourced conquest and colonial exploitation (including atrocities such as the 1621 Banda massacre) to a joint-stock corporation answerable to its shareholders. The merchant-financier class governed the Republic.

### XVII. The Great Divergence and the Rise of Britain — London, Slavery, and the Industrial Revolution (c. 1700–1900)
The hub then moved across the North Sea to London, where a uniquely potent combination of mechanisms compounded. Dominant: COMMERCE (finance + later manufacturing); heavy secondary: WAR-TRANSFER (Atlantic slavery + colonial extraction) and EXTRACTION (coal).

First came the Financial Revolution: the founding of the Bank of England (1694) and a market in sovereign debt (British public debt became, in effect, the world's first global "safe asset"), giving Britain an unmatched ability to borrow and wage war. Second came the Atlantic triangular trade and slavery: in the thesis associated with Eric Williams (Capitalism and Slavery, 1944), profits from the slave trade and from slave-produced sugar, tobacco, and cotton helped capitalize British industrialization — Williams argued "it was the capital accumulated from the West Indian trade that financed James Watt and the steam engine," and that the slave ports of Bristol, Liverpool, and Glasgow and the manufacturing of Manchester were nodes of a single Atlantic slavery economy. (Williams' strong causal claim is contested by economic historians, who debate how large the slave-trade contribution to total British investment really was, while generally accepting that specific industrial fortunes were built on slave wealth.) Third came colonial extraction from India: after Plassey (1757), the East India Company converted Bengal's land revenue and textile wealth into a tribute flow, and India was progressively deindustrialized — its share of world manufacturing output collapsing from ~25% in 1750 to ~2% by 1900 even as Britain's economy soared.

The culmination was the Industrial Revolution, which added a genuinely new mechanism of capital accumulation: sustained productivity growth from coal-powered manufacturing. Kenneth Pomeranz's The Great Divergence (2000) argues that Britain and the most advanced parts of Asia (the Yangzi Delta) were at broadly comparable living standards until around 1750–1800, and that Britain pulled ahead because of two contingent advantages: (1) coal conveniently located near its industrial centers (China's coal was in the far northwest, distant from the Yangzi textile heartland — Pomeranz notes the Yangzi region itself held merely ~1.8% of China's coal reserves), and (2) the New World "ghost acreage" — colonies and slave economies that supplied land-saving cotton, sugar, and timber, relieving Britain's ecological constraints. (The "California School" dating is contested: Stephen Broadberry and colleagues, using national-accounting reconstructions, argue Britain had already edged ahead of the Yangzi Delta by the early 1700s; the emerging consensus places the crossover in the 18th century.) By the 19th century the divergence was total — and it was sealed by force in the Opium Wars: to reverse the centuries-old silver drain into China (paid for tea, silk, and porcelain), Britain used the EIC to flood China with Indian-grown opium; opium reached ~57% of Chinese imports by 1837; and after the First Opium War (1839–42), the Treaty of Nanjing imposed a 21-million-silver-dollar indemnity, opened five treaty ports, and ceded Hong Kong — the start of China's "century of humiliation." (Recent econometric work by Irigoin argues the silver-drain magnitude was substantially smaller than the polemical contemporary figures of Lin Zexu and others suggested.)

By the late 19th century, the City of London was the financial capital of the world: more than half of world trade was financed in sterling, the gold standard centered on London, and British net foreign investment reached roughly £2.4 billion by 1900. London supplied capital to the entire planet — Argentine railways, American bonds, South African mines.

Capital–power relationship: finance disciplines the state, and the state serves finance. Britain perfected the fusion of a constitutional state with deep capital markets: the state's creditworthiness funded its navy, and the navy (Pax Britannica) protected the trade and investment that fed the City. Banking dynasties like the Rothschilds became powerful enough to finance governments and influence war and peace.

### XVIII. The American Century — New York, Wall Street, and the Dollar System (c. 1900–2000)
Capital crossed the Atlantic a final time to the United States, above all to New York. Dominant: COMMERCE (industry + finance); decisive secondary: WAR-TRANSFER (the World Wars as a wealth transfer to the U.S.). America first built immense industrial wealth (steel, oil, railroads), but the financial crown passed via the two World Wars. World War I turned the U.S. from a net debtor into the world's largest creditor: before 1917, J.P. Morgan & Co. served as Britain's purchasing agent and organized Allied loans — including the 1915 Anglo-French loan of $500 million, the largest foreign loan in Wall Street history to that point (per PBS American Experience, J.P. Morgan "would eventually secure a $500 million dollar line of credit for the French and British — the biggest foreign loan in Wall Street history"). And per the IMF (Debt and Entanglements Between the Wars), "Between 1917 and 1920, the US War and Navy departments spent $20 billion and the Treasury extended $9.5 billion in loans to Britain, France, Italy, and eight other allies." (Eichengreen and Flandreau have shown the dollar first overtook sterling as the leading reserve currency as early as the mid-1920s, lost the lead after the 1933 dollar devaluation, and took it permanently in the early 1940s.) World War II finished the job: by the Bretton Woods conference of July 1944, the U.S. held about two-thirds of the world's monetary gold (per the Federal Reserve History essay and as Wikipedia summarizes, "The United States, which controlled two-thirds of the world's gold, insisted that the Bretton Woods system rest on both gold and the US dollar"), and the resulting system made the dollar the world's reserve currency, fixed to gold at $35/oz with all other currencies pegged to the dollar — the "exorbitant privilege." Even after Nixon ended gold convertibility in 1971, the dollar's centrality was reinforced by the petrodollar arrangement: from 1974 Saudi Arabia and other Gulf producers priced oil in dollars and recycled their surpluses into U.S. Treasuries and Western assets.

Capital–power relationship: financial markets as a global governing force. By the late 20th century, the relationship had fully inverted from the temple-state: sovereign states now answered to bond markets and a dollar-based financial system centered on Wall Street and the Federal Reserve. After Margaret Thatcher's 1986 "Big Bang" deregulation, New York and a revived London became the twin "Alpha" poles of a globalized financial system that could discipline governments.

### XIX. The Re-Rise of Asia and the New Nodes — Tokyo, the Tigers, China, the Gulf, and Silicon Valley (c. 1950–2026)
The most important recent development is the return of economic gravity toward Asia, reversing the Great Divergence. Dominant mechanism for the East Asian model: COMMERCE (export manufacturing), organized by a developmental state. Japan's postwar miracle (guided by MITI and the bank-centered keiretsu) made Tokyo the world's second-largest economy by the 1970s; the Four Asian Tigers (Hong Kong, Singapore, South Korea, Taiwan) followed via export-led industrialization. Most consequentially, China's reform era — Deng Xiaoping's 1978 reforms, the Shenzhen Special Economic Zone (1980), and the opening of Shanghai's Pudong (1990) — drove an economy that grew roughly 40-fold by 2020 and became the world's second-largest in 2010, re-establishing China as a top-two global economy for the first time since the early 19th century.

Two other modern node-types stand out. The Gulf petro-states (Saudi Arabia, UAE, Kuwait, Qatar) represent pure modern EXTRACTION converted into financial power: their sovereign wealth funds (the Kuwait Investment Authority, Abu Dhabi Investment Authority, Saudi PIF, Qatar Investment Authority) recycle oil surpluses into global assets, with GCC funds holding well over $2 trillion in U.S. assets alone. And Silicon Valley is the leading node of capital accumulation through COMMERCE in its most modern form — technology and venture capital. Built on the semiconductor lineage (Shockley → Fairchild → Intel) and the venture-capital model pioneered on Sand Hill Road from 1972 (Kleiner Perkins, Sequoia), it concentrates capital through self-reinforcing network effects of talent, research universities, and risk capital — such that a handful of U.S. technology companies now rank among the most valuable enterprises in history. Nvidia, on Wednesday, October 29, 2025, became the first company in history to reach a $5 trillion market capitalization (per Axios: "Nvidia surged at the market open Wednesday, becoming the first company in history to be worth more than $5 trillion," with a real-time cap around $5.05 trillion).

Capital–power relationship: two divergent models. East Asia revived the old Chinese pattern of a developmental state fused with large firms (the state picks strategic sectors; firms execute), while Silicon Valley represents the apotheosis of the autonomous private capital class — venture investors and founders whose wealth derives from equity in scalable intellectual property, largely separate from (and increasingly able to influence) political power. The Gulf model fuses sovereign and capital power once more, as ruling families deploy national resource wealth as a strategic financial instrument.

## Recommendations
(Framed as guidance for a reader using this timeline analytically.)

- **To predict where the next hub forms, watch the mechanism mix, not the map.** History's most durable hubs combined COMMERCE with a financial innovation that let capital compound (Amsterdam's stock exchange, London's sovereign-debt market, New York's dollar system). Pure-EXTRACTION hubs (Spain/Potosí, arguably today's petro-states) generate spectacular windfalls but decay unless the rents are converted into commerce, manufacturing, or financial infrastructure. The benchmark to watch: is a rising node building reinvestable financial institutions, or merely selling a finite resource?
- **Track the capital–power relationship as a leading indicator.** When an autonomous financial class begins to dictate political outcomes (Medici Florence, Rothschild-era London, modern Wall Street/Big Tech), a node is at or near its peak influence. When the state must instead repress or expropriate its capitalists, the node's compounding engine is stalling.
- **Treat the current moment as a partial reversal of the Great Divergence, not a clean handover.** China's re-rise restores the pre-1800 norm of Asian economic weight, but the reserve-currency and deep-capital-markets advantages still sit with the U.S. (and secondarily London). The historical lesson from the sterling-to-dollar transition is that reserve-currency shifts take decades and typically require a credible alternative with deep, liquid capital markets plus a catalysing crisis — so monitor the depth and openness of rival capital markets, not just GDP rankings.
- **Thresholds that would change the assessment:** a rival currency bloc achieving genuinely deep, freely convertible capital markets; a sustained reversal in the share of global trade invoiced in dollars; or the emergence of a new general-purpose technology (as coal/steam was) that concentrates productivity gains in a specific new geography.

## Caveats
- **Historical GDP numbers are estimates, not measurements.** Angus Maddison's figures (and the Maddison Project's updates) are heroic reconstructions, openly criticized by other economic historians (e.g., for early-medieval India and for the 1990-vs-2011 PPP benchmarks). The "China was ~33% of world GDP in 1820" and "India ~25% of world manufacturing in 1750" figures convey real and important magnitudes but should be read as scholarly best-guesses with wide error bars.
- **The "single dominant hub" framing is a simplification.** For long stretches the world had multiple co-equal poles (Rome and Han China; Abbasid Baghdad, Song China, and Mughal-era India). The "throughline" is a useful narrative device, not a claim that one city ruled the global economy at any given instant.
- **Contested causal claims are flagged in the text.** The Eric Williams thesis (slavery → Industrial Revolution), the magnitude of the Opium-era silver drain (recent work by Irigoin argues it is greatly overstated), the dating of the dollar's overtaking of sterling (mid-1920s "new view" vs. post-WWII traditional view), and the precise dating of the Great Divergence (Pomeranz's ~1800 vs. Broadberry's early-1700s) are all genuine scholarly debates, not settled facts.
- **Some figures are contemporary or moralizing sources.** Pliny's claim about Rome's trade deficit with the East, and the most dramatic accounts of Mansa Musa's gold or Potosí's death tolls, derive from sources prone to exaggeration; they are reported here as illustrative, with that caveat.
- **Real-time market-capitalization figures** (e.g., Nvidia's October 2025 $5 trillion milestone) fluctuate and reflect their stated date; they are included to illustrate the scale of the technology node, not as fixed values.
