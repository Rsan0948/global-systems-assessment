# Polity-Shock Observations via Shock-Cohort Sampling (1900–2015)

## Methodology note
This report applies SHOCK-COHORT sampling: rather than hand-picking famous crises, I built a numbered pool of multi-country shock waves, RNG-drew a subset, and then took the membership of each drawn cohort (all members, or a documented random subset), reporting outcomes regardless of whether they were dramatic or unremarkable. No scoring, rating, or interpretation is applied; only neutral facts and the two tags (origin; outcome). Survivors are reported alongside casualties.

### Numbered shock pool (≥20 multi-country shock events/waves, 1900–2015)
1. Great Depression in Latin America (1929–32) — internal-leaning (global price collapse transmitted to commodity exporters)
2. 1982 Latin American/LDC debt crisis — internal
3. 1994 CFA franc devaluation (franc zone) — internal
4. 1994–95 Tequila crisis spillover — internal
5. 1997 Asian financial crisis — internal
6. 2008 global financial crisis — internal
7. Eurozone sovereign-debt crisis (2010–12) — internal
8. 2014–16 oil-price collapse — external/mixed
9. 1986 oil-price collapse — external/mixed
10. 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami — external
11. 2014–16 West Africa Ebola epidemic — external
12. 1918–20 influenza pandemic — external
13. 1930s commodity collapse in Africa/Asia colonies — external
14. WWII occupations (1939–45) — external
15. 1973 oil shock — external
16. 2010–11 Arab Spring uprising wave — internal
17. Post-Soviet/Yugoslav transitions (1989–92) — internal
18. 1980s African one-party-state fiscal crises — internal
19. 2015–16 commodity crash (metals) — external/mixed
20. 1907 financial panic — internal
21. 1998 Russian financial crisis spillover — internal
22. 1991–94 sub-Saharan democratization wave — internal

### RNG draw (documented)
Using a random number generator over 1–22, the drawn shocks were: **1 (Great Depression LatAm), 2 (1982 debt crisis), 3 (1994 CFA devaluation), 4 (Tequila 1994–95), 8 (2014–16 oil collapse), 10 (2004 tsunami), 11 (2014–16 Ebola)**. This yields six internally-originating cohorts and two externally-originating cohorts (tsunami, Ebola) plus the mixed-origin oil cohort, satisfying the coverage rule (≥half internal, ≥quarter external). Within cohorts larger than ~8, a random subset was drawn and documented. Excluded already-used members were dropped and the remaining cohort members retained.

---

## TL;DR
- **Across eight drawn cohorts, the dominant outcome was survival, not collapse**: of the 27 counted polity-shock observations, the single most common tag is "Stressed but continuous" (≈16 cases). Dramatic ruptures cluster heavily in the 1929–32 Great Depression cohort (Peru, Bolivia, El Salvador all suffered violent rupture), while most post-1995 cohorts (oil 2014–16, Ebola 2014–16, tsunami 2004) saw incumbent regimes absorb the shock and persist.
- **Origin does not determine severity**: externally-originating shocks ranged from state-stabilizing absorption (Seychelles and Senegal each shrugged off their shock) to compounding fragility (Somalia, Sri Lanka), while internally-originating debt/currency shocks were survived by every 1982 and 1994 cohort member documented here, with only the Great Depression producing coups and massacres.
- **The pattern that best predicts rupture is pre-shock political fragility, not the shock's size**: stable democracies and entrenched autocracies alike weathered large economic blows (Costa Rica, Cameroon, Kazakhstan, Angola), whereas already-contested or newly-installed regimes ruptured (Peru 1930, Bolivia 1930, El Salvador 1931, Côte d'Ivoire 1999).

## Key Findings
- **Outcome distribution (27 counted observations)**: Collapse/fragmentation (1) — none cleanly in this draw; Violent rupture (2) — Peru, Bolivia, El Salvador (3); Peaceful discontinuity (3) — Chile, Philippines, Benin (≈3); Stressed but continuous (4) — Costa Rica, Dominican Republic, Jamaica, Côte d'Ivoire (rupture-flagged), Cameroon, Mali, Argentina, Angola, Azerbaijan, Kazakhstan, Congo, Gabon, Equatorial Guinea, Maldives, Sri Lanka, Somalia, Sierra Leone, Liberia, Guinea (the large majority); Absorbed/stable (5) — Seychelles, Senegal (2).
- **The Great Depression cohort is the outlier for severity**: four of four Latin American members experienced regime change within ~2 years, three of them violent. This contrasts sharply with the 2014–16 oil cohort, where six of six oil exporters retained their incumbent regimes despite deep recessions and currency collapses.
- **Currency/disease shocks were routinely absorbed**: the 1994 CFA devaluation produced economic *recovery* in most members (Mali ≈5% average annual growth; Cameroon ≈5% to 1997/98), and the West Africa Ebola epidemic, though economically crippling for the three core countries, did not topple a single government.
- **A skeptical counterpoint on the CFA "success" narrative**: a 2022 peer-reviewed synthetic-control study finds that, except for Mali, statistically rigorous per-capita GDP gains versus the counterfactual are weak across the cohort — so the headline IMF/World Bank growth figures should be read with caution.

## Details

### COHORT A — Great Depression in Latin America (onset 1929–30)
*Origin: Internal-leaning/mixed — a global collapse in export commodity prices transmitted through dependent primary-export economies, triggering domestic fiscal and political crises.*

**A1. Chile (CHL), South America**
- *Shock*: Collapse of nitrate and copper export markets from 1930, the deepest in Latin America (onset 1930).
- *Origin tag*: Mixed — external price collapse channeled through Chile's export-dependent economy; the League of Nations rated Chile the worst-affected nation.
- *Pre-shock snapshot (~1927–29)*: Chile was governed by the authoritarian administration of Carlos Ibáñez del Campo, financed heavily by foreign (largely US) loans and nitrate/copper revenues. The Central Bank, created in 1925, operated under the gold standard. Exports stood around US$279 million in 1929. Mining employed roughly 6% of the active population but dominated foreign-exchange earnings.
- *Outcome tag*: **3 Peaceful discontinuity** (bordering violent) — Ibáñez fell in July 1931; a rapid succession of short-lived governments and the brief "Socialist Republic" (June–September 1932) followed, before Arturo Alessandri was elected in December 1932 and constitutional normality returned. Exports had collapsed to about US$35 million by 1932; copper recovered to roughly pre-Depression levels by 1937. Key date: July 1931 (fall of Ibáñez).
- *Era flag*: 1900–1949
- *Sources*: Wikipedia, "Great Depression in Chile"; Britannica, "Chile — Effects of the world depression"; SciELO, "The Great Depression: A Defining Moment in Chile's Development?"

**A2. Peru (PER), South America**
- *Shock*: Collapse of export prices (sugar, cotton, minerals) and foreign lending from 1930 (onset 1930).
- *Origin tag*: Mixed — external commodity/credit collapse triggering a domestic fiscal and leadership crisis.
- *Pre-shock snapshot (~1927–29)*: Peru was in the eleventh year of Augusto B. Leguía's authoritarian *Oncenio*, sustained by heavy US borrowing and public works. The economy depended on sugar, cotton and mineral exports. By 1930 Leguía had lost popularity as the global crisis drained foreign investment.
- *Outcome tag*: **2 Violent rupture** — Leguía was overthrown in the August 1930 Arequipa coup led by Luis Sánchez Cerro; Peru defaulted on external debt in 1931; Sánchez Cerro won the 1931 election; the 1932 Aprista Trujillo uprising was crushed with mass executions, and Sánchez Cerro was assassinated in 1933. Key date: 22–25 August 1930 (coup).
- *Era flag*: 1900–1949
- *Sources*: Wikipedia, "1930 Peruvian coup d'état" and "1931 Peruvian general election"; Britannica, "Peru — Formation of the Aprista movement"; IMF WP/12/166, "Peru: Monetary and Exchange Rate Policies, 1930–1980."

**A3. Bolivia (BOL), South America**
- *Shock*: Collapse of tin prices and revenues from 1930 (onset 1930).
- *Origin tag*: Mixed — external tin-price collapse transmitted to a mining monoculture, fueling fiscal distress and political instability.
- *Pre-shock snapshot (~1927–29)*: Bolivia was ruled by Republican governments closely tied to the tin-mining magnates (the *rosca*). Tin was the principal source of revenue. President Hernando Siles Reyes governed amid rising labor and student unrest; the Central Bank was founded in 1929.
- *Outcome tag*: **2 Violent rupture** — Siles Reyes was ousted in June 1930; the tin collapse pushed Bolivia deep into debt by 1932; the mining industry alone laid off about 20,000 workers (≈26,600 across sectors, 1930–32); and President Daniel Salamanca led Bolivia into the catastrophic Chaco War with Paraguay (1932–35), which it lost (≈56,000–65,000 Bolivian dead), discrediting the ruling class. Key date: June 1930 (fall of Siles Reyes); war onset 1932.
- *Era flag*: 1900–1949
- *Sources*: Wikipedia, "History of Bolivia (1920–1964)" and "Chaco War"; SciELO, "The foundation of the Central Bank of Bolivia (1929–1932)"; Britannica, "Chaco War."

**A4. El Salvador (SLV), Central America**
- *Shock*: Collapse of coffee prices after the 1929 crash (onset 1931).
- *Origin tag*: Mixed — external coffee-price collapse intensifying domestic class/ethnic conflict and a leadership crisis.
- *Pre-shock snapshot (~1928–30)*: El Salvador's economy rested on coffee exports controlled by a narrow oligarchy (the "fourteen families"); the 1927–31 liberalization under Pío Romero Bosque produced the relatively free 1931 election won by reformist Arturo Araujo. Coffee prices fell steeply after 1929, and landowners cut wages.
- *Outcome tag*: **2 Violent rupture** — Araujo was overthrown in a December 1931 military coup installing Gen. Maximiliano Hernández Martínez; a January 1932 peasant/communist uprising was suppressed in *La Matanza*. Per the Encyclopedia of Latin American History, estimates "vary widely, running from 3,000 to 30,000," with "a reasonable guess [of] about 10,000 killed"; commonly cited ranges run to ~40,000 (up to 4% of the population), inaugurating decades of military rule. Key date: January 1932 (La Matanza).
- *Era flag*: 1900–1949
- *Sources*: Wikipedia, "La Matanza"; Encyclopedia.com, "Matanza"; Zinn Education Project, "La Matanza."

### COHORT B — 1982 Latin American/LDC debt crisis (onset 1982)
*Origin: Internal — sovereign-debt/balance-of-payments crises triggered by accumulated external borrowing meeting a surge in global interest rates and the August 1982 Mexican default.*

**B1. Costa Rica (CRI), Central America**
- *Shock*: Debt crisis and balance-of-payments collapse, onset 1981–82.
- *Origin tag*: Internal — overborrowing and fiscal/monetary mismanagement under a fixed exchange rate, aggravated by external interest rates and terms-of-trade shocks.
- *Pre-shock snapshot (~1979–80)*: Costa Rica was a stable social democracy with high public spending (total public spending ≈54% of GDP by 1980) and many state enterprises. President Rodrigo Carazo borrowed heavily to defend a fixed exchange rate; external debt quadrupled. The current-account deficit reached 12.6% of GDP in 1980.
- *Outcome tag*: **4 Stressed but continuous** — between 1980 and 1982 the economy contracted by 9.4% and 1982 inflation reached about 90%, with poverty rising above 50%; but democratic institutions survived, IMF stabilization began in 1982, and the country transitioned to an export-oriented model without a democratic breakdown. Key date: 1982 (peak crisis / first IMF letter of intent).
- *Era flag*: 1950–1995
- *Sources*: Cato Institute, "Growth without Poverty Reduction: The Case of Costa Rica"; NACLA, "Costa Rica: The Non-Market Roots of Market Success"; Berkeley (Rodríguez-Clare), "Economic Growth in Costa Rica: 1950–2000."

**B2. Philippines (PHL), Southeast Asia**
- *Shock*: Sovereign debt crisis and capital-flight crisis, onset 1983.
- *Origin tag*: Internal — debt accumulation and a political-legitimacy crisis under the Marcos dictatorship, transmitted by the regional debt shock.
- *Pre-shock snapshot (~1980–82)*: The Philippines was under the Marcos martial-law/authoritarian regime, with heavy external borrowing, crony-dominated industry, and rising fiscal strain; the economy was vulnerable to the global rise in dollar interest rates.
- *Outcome tag*: **3 Peaceful discontinuity** — the debt shock (compounded by the August 1983 Aquino assassination and capital flight) produced a severe 1984–85 recession with lasting consequences; the Marcos regime fell in the February 1986 People Power revolution, restoring democracy under Corazon Aquino. Key date: 1983 (debt moratorium / Aquino assassination).
- *Era flag*: 1950–1995
- *Sources*: Wiley, Punongbayan (2026), "Debt, Dictatorship, and Decline: The Enduring Economic Impact of the Philippines' 1980s Crisis"; Federal Reserve History, "Latin American Debt Crisis of the 1980s."

**B3. Dominican Republic (DOM), Caribbean**
- *Shock*: Debt crisis and IMF austerity, onset 1982–84.
- *Origin tag*: Internal — debt-driven fiscal crisis and IMF adjustment producing domestic unrest.
- *Pre-shock snapshot (~1980–82)*: The Dominican Republic was an emerging democracy under the PRD, having achieved its first peaceful transfer of power in 1978. Salvador Jorge Blanco won the 1982 election. The economy, dependent on sugar exports, faced a growing debt burden.
- *Outcome tag*: **4 Stressed but continuous** — an April 1984 IMF austerity program triggered riots that killed more than 50 people; democracy survived (Balaguer returned via the 1986 election), though a 1988–90 depression with high inflation followed. Key date: April 1984 (austerity riots).
- *Era flag*: 1950–1995
- *Sources*: U.S. State Department, "Dominican Republic (10/00)"; Washington Post (Apr 30, 1984), "Price Riots Peril Dominican Government"; BTI 2026 Dominican Republic Country Report.

**B4. Jamaica (JAM), Caribbean**
- *Shock*: Debt and structural-adjustment crisis, onset early 1980s.
- *Origin tag*: Internal — external debt overhang (rooted in 1970s oil shocks) meeting rising interest rates, producing chronic austerity.
- *Pre-shock snapshot (~1979–81)*: Jamaica, dependent on bauxite/alumina and cash crops, had run up heavy foreign debt after the 1970s oil shocks. Debt payments rose from 16% of government revenue in 1977 toward 40% by 1984.
- *Outcome tag*: **4 Stressed but continuous** — the country entered prolonged IMF structural adjustment and austerity through the 1980s (registered nurses fell ≈60% over the decade), with debt payments staying above 20% of revenue, but constitutional democracy persisted. Key date: 1984 (debt-service peak / adjustment).
- *Era flag*: 1950–1995
- *Sources*: Debt Justice (Jubilee), "Jamaica"; CEPR, "Jamaica: Macroeconomic Policy, Debt and the IMF."

### COHORT C — 1994 CFA franc devaluation (onset 12 January 1994)
*Origin: Internal — a 50% currency devaluation imposed across the franc zone (50→100 CFA per French franc), a policy/monetary shock common to all members.*

**C1. Côte d'Ivoire (CIV), West Africa**
- *Shock*: 50% CFA devaluation, January 1994.
- *Origin tag*: Internal — a region-wide monetary-policy decision; Côte d'Ivoire was the largest WAEMU economy (≈40% of output).
- *Pre-shock snapshot (~1991–93)*: Côte d'Ivoire was in prolonged recession after years of falling commodity terms of trade, with the CFA increasingly overvalued. Founding President Félix Houphouët-Boigny died on 7 December 1993, succeeded constitutionally by Henri Konan Bédié.
- *Outcome tag*: **2 Violent rupture** — the devaluation produced strong growth (about 5% annually in 1996–99 per official statistics) and reversed the macro decline, but the post-shock decade ended in rupture: the first coup in Ivorian history on 24 December 1999 (Gen. Robert Guéï deposing Bédié), followed by disputed 2000 elections and civil war from 2002. Key date: 24 December 1999 (coup).
- *Era flag*: 1950–1995 (onset); aftermath spans post-1996
- *Sources*: IMF WP/05/145, "Ten Years After the CFA Franc Devaluation"; U.S. State Department 2000 Country Commercial Guide; Wikipedia, "1999 Ivorian coup d'état."

**C2. Cameroon (CMR), Central Africa**
- *Shock*: 50% CFA devaluation, January 1994.
- *Origin tag*: Internal — region-wide monetary shock.
- *Pre-shock snapshot (~1991–93)*: Cameroon had endured roughly eight years of economic decline; real GDP had fallen by an annual average of about 4% since the mid-1980s, and real per-capita GDP had fallen more than 60% from 1986. Paul Biya, president since 1982, had survived a disputed 1992 multiparty election.
- *Outcome tag*: **4 Stressed but continuous** — output recovered, with annual GDP growth stabilizing at about 5% in the three years to 1997/98 (IMF), while inflation spiked near 35% in 1994; politically, the Biya regime persisted (re-elected 1997 with 92.6%). Key date: 1994 (devaluation) / 1997 (re-election).
- *Era flag*: 1950–1995 (onset); aftermath post-1996
- *Sources*: IMF, "Cameroon: Selected Issues and Statistical Appendix"; IMF Survey, "Cameroon: What Next After Debt Relief?"; Wikipedia, "Paul Biya."

**C3. Benin (BEN), West Africa**
- *Shock*: 50% CFA devaluation, January 1994.
- *Origin tag*: Internal — region-wide monetary shock.
- *Pre-shock snapshot (~1991–93)*: Benin had just pioneered Africa's democratic transition (Nicéphore Soglo elected 1991, after the 1990 national conference). The economy depended on cotton and re-export trade with Nigeria, and had been under structural adjustment since 1989.
- *Outcome tag*: **3 Peaceful discontinuity** (with continuity of the democratic system) — the devaluation accelerated growth and improved the balance of payments (IMF); democracy held, with former military ruler Mathieu Kérékou defeating incumbent Soglo (52.5% in the 18 March 1996 runoff) in a peaceful ballot-box alternation. Key date: 18 March 1996 (election).
- *Era flag*: 1950–1995 (onset); aftermath post-1996
- *Sources*: IMF, "Benin: Enhanced Structural Adjustment Facility Policy Framework Paper"; Brookings, on Benin's democratic transitions; Wikipedia, "1996 Beninese presidential election."

**C4. Mali (MLI), West Africa**
- *Shock*: 50% CFA devaluation, January 1994.
- *Origin tag*: Internal — region-wide monetary shock.
- *Pre-shock snapshot (~1991–93)*: Mali had just transitioned to democracy after the 1991 coup that ousted Moussa Traoré; Alpha Oumar Konaré was elected and sworn in June 1992. The economy was agrarian, import-dependent, with little to export.
- *Outcome tag*: **4 Stressed but continuous** — Mali's economy grew at an average annual rate of about 5% after the 1994 devaluation (World Bank), the strongest documented per-capita gain in the cohort; inflation neared 35% in 1994 before moderating; democratic continuity held, with Konaré re-elected in 1997 and serving two full terms (1992–2002). Key date: 1994 (devaluation) / 1997 (re-election).
- *Era flag*: 1950–1995 (onset); aftermath post-1996
- *Sources*: World Bank Independent Evaluation Group (Mali education evaluation); Eastern Economic Journal (Anderson et al., 2022), "Currency Devaluation as a Source of Growth in Africa"; Wikipedia, "Alpha Oumar Konaré."

### COHORT D — 1994–95 Tequila crisis spillover (onset December 1994)
*Origin: Internal — a currency/financial crisis originating in Mexico's December 1994 peso devaluation, transmitted via capital flight to other emerging markets ("Tequila effect").*

**D1. Argentina (ARG), South America**
- *Shock*: Tequila-effect capital flight and banking crisis, onset December 1994–March 1995.
- *Origin tag*: External (to Argentina) — exogenous capital flight triggered by Mexico's peso collapse, hitting Argentina's currency-board system. (Distinct from the excluded "Argentina 2001" episode.)
- *Pre-shock snapshot (~1992–94)*: Argentina operated a currency board (Convertibility) pegging the peso to the dollar, having tamed hyperinflation; the financial system had liberalized and attracted capital inflows under President Carlos Menem.
- *Outcome tag*: **4 Stressed but continuous** — the 1995 shock produced a credit crunch, an interest-rate spike (March 1995), a banking shake-out and a sharp output decline, but the currency board held, the system was recapitalized, and Menem was re-elected in 1995. Key date: March 1995 (peak crisis).
- *Era flag*: 1950–1995
- *Sources*: Federal Reserve IFDP, "The Tequila Effect: Theory and Evidence from Argentina"; AEI, "Lessons from the Tequila Crisis for Successful Financial Liberalization."

*Cohort note: After applying the hard constraints, this cohort's open membership is thin (Mexico is the origin and restricted to pre-1990 episodes; Brazil is on the pre-1990-only list; Chile and Venezuela were "relatively unaffected" per the AEI source). Mexico (origin) and Brazil are therefore noted only for completeness and are NOT counted among the ~30 observations. The remaining observation budget is reallocated to the larger open cohorts (E, F, G) under the documented random-subset rule — done explicitly rather than silently.*

### COHORT E — 2014–16 oil-price collapse (onset mid-2014)
*Origin: Mixed/external — a global crude-price collapse hitting oil-dependent exporters; transmitted externally but acting through each state's internal fiscal structure. Per IMF WP/17/160 and the World Bank, "the Brent price of crude oil declined from $112 in June 2014 to a low of $31 in January 2016," a fall of more than 70% — "one of the three biggest declines since World War II, and the longest lasting since the supply-driven collapse of 1986."*

**E1. Angola (AGO), Southern Africa**
- *Shock*: Oil-price collapse from mid-2014.
- *Origin tag*: External/mixed — global price shock acting on an economy where oil was ≈95% of exports.
- *Pre-shock snapshot (~2011–13)*: Angola was sub-Saharan Africa's second-largest oil producer, with a decade of rapid (often double-digit) growth since the 2002 end of civil war, an entrenched MPLA/dos Santos regime, large infrastructure spending, and a sovereign wealth fund; reserves were high but the economy was undiversified.
- *Outcome tag*: **4 Stressed but continuous** — the collapse drove a multi-year recession (from 2016), sharp currency depreciation, debt-to-GDP above 120% by end-2020, and austerity; but the MPLA regime held, managing a constitutional leadership handover from dos Santos to João Lourenço in 2017. Key date: 2016 (recession onset).
- *Era flag*: post-1996
- *Sources*: IMF Country Report No. 15/302 (Angola); Brookings, "Angola: Navigating turbulent times"; CMI, "Angola from boom to bust."

**E2. Azerbaijan (AZE), Caucasus**
- *Shock*: Oil-price collapse from mid-2014.
- *Origin tag*: External/mixed — global price shock; hydrocarbons were roughly three-quarters of export income.
- *Pre-shock snapshot (~2011–13)*: Azerbaijan was an oil-dependent autocracy under Ilham Aliyev, with a stable dollar-pegged manat, high foreign reserves (US$15.2 billion in mid-2014), and a sovereign wealth fund (SOFAZ); civil liberties were tightly restricted.
- *Outcome tag*: **4 Stressed but continuous** — per RFE/RL, "the Central Bank devalued the manat in mid-February 2015 by 33.5 percent vis-à-vis the U.S. dollar [and] the manat lost a further 47 percent in value in December 2015" (when it was floated); FX reserves fell from US$15.2 billion to US$5.1 billion (July 2014–December 2015), inflation hit double digits, and localized protests occurred; the Aliyev regime held and later restored macro stability. Key date: 21 February / 21 December 2015 (devaluations).
- *Era flag*: post-1996
- *Sources*: CACI Analyst, "Azerbaijan and Kazakhstan Face Tough Economic Decisions"; IWPR, "Azerbaijanis Struggle After Currency Devaluation"; World Economic Forum (2025), "Azerbaijan's journey from economic crisis to confidence."

**E3. Kazakhstan (KAZ), Central Asia**
- *Shock*: Oil-price collapse from mid-2014.
- *Origin tag*: External/mixed — global price shock plus devaluations by trading partners Russia and China.
- *Pre-shock snapshot (~2011–13)*: Kazakhstan was an oil-and-metals exporter under longtime president Nursultan Nazarbayev, with a managed tenge, large national oil fund and reserves exceeding US$100 billion, and a stable authoritarian system.
- *Outcome tag*: **4 Stressed but continuous** — the tenge was floated in August 2015 and lost roughly 26% initially (and far more over six months, ~78.5% against the dollar after six months per University of Liverpool research), driving inflation; the Nazarbayev regime remained firmly in control. Key date: 20 August 2015 (float).
- *Era flag*: post-1996
- *Sources*: University of Liverpool, "How a currency devaluation in Kazakhstan made the country's poor worse off"; CACI Analyst, "Azerbaijan and Kazakhstan Face Tough Economic Decisions"; MDPI, "The Impact of Oil Price Shocks on Oil-Dependent Countries' Currencies."

**E4. Republic of the Congo (COG), Central Africa**
- *Shock*: Oil-price collapse from mid-2014.
- *Origin tag*: External/mixed — global price shock on a CEMAC oil exporter.
- *Pre-shock snapshot (~2011–13)*: Congo-Brazzaville was an oil-dependent state under Denis Sassou Nguesso (in power since 1979 bar 1992–97), with oil-financed autocratic stability and entrenched ethnoregional divisions.
- *Outcome tag*: **4 Stressed but continuous** (with localized violence) — the price collapse caused fiscal crisis, rising debt and recession; Sassou Nguesso changed the constitution to extend rule and won a disputed March 2016 election, after which renewed fighting flared in the Pool region (2016–17). Key date: March 2016 (disputed election).
- *Era flag*: post-1996
- *Sources*: BTI 2026 Congo, Rep. Country Report; IFRI, "The Economic and Political Consequences of Falling Oil Production in Sub-Saharan Africa"; ODI, "The oil price shock of 2014."

**E5. Gabon (GAB), Central Africa**
- *Shock*: Oil-price collapse from mid-2014.
- *Origin tag*: External/mixed — global price shock on a CEMAC oil exporter.
- *Pre-shock snapshot (~2011–13)*: Gabon was an upper-middle-income oil exporter under Ali Bongo Ondimba (succeeded his father Omar in 2009), with oil-dependent public finances and a half-century Bongo-family dominance.
- *Outcome tag*: **4 Stressed but continuous** — annual output growth slowed (from 2.1% in 2014 to 1.3% in 2015 per IMF); the August 2016 disputed re-election of Bongo triggered deadly protests, the burning of the National Assembly, and roughly 1,100 arrests, but the regime survived. Key date: 31 August 2016 (post-election violence).
- *Era flag*: post-1996
- *Sources*: Wikipedia, "2016 Gabonese protests"; NPR, "Deadly Protests Shake Gabon"; Taylor & Francis, "Crude oil price shocks and macroeconomic performance in Africa's oil-producing countries."

**E6. Equatorial Guinea (GNQ), Central Africa**
- *Shock*: Oil-price collapse from mid-2014.
- *Origin tag*: External/mixed — global price shock on a small, highly oil-dependent exporter.
- *Pre-shock snapshot (~2011–13)*: Equatorial Guinea had been the fastest-growing economy in the world at its oil-boom peak, with GDP per capita on par with industrialized nations but extreme inequality and a kleptocratic system under Teodoro Obiang; it was spending lavishly on prestige projects (e.g., the new capital Ciudad de la Paz).
- *Outcome tag*: **4 Stressed but continuous** — dwindling oil revenue after 2014 stalled the prestige-project program (the new capital's construction ground to a halt) and the economy contracted, but the Obiang regime persisted. Key date: 2014–16 (revenue collapse).
- *Era flag*: post-1996
- *Sources*: ICIJ (2025), "As Equatorial Guinea burned through oil riches…"; IFRI, "The Economic and Political Consequences of Falling Oil Production in Sub-Saharan Africa"; IMF WP/17/160, "Explaining the Impact of the 2014–16 Oil Price Decline."

### COHORT F — 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami (onset 26 December 2004)
*Origin: External — a sudden natural disaster (magnitude 9.1 earthquake off Sumatra and the resulting tsunami) striking many coastal states simultaneously.*

**F1. Maldives (MDV), South Asia/Indian Ocean**
- *Shock*: Tsunami, 26 December 2004.
- *Origin tag*: External — natural disaster originating ~2,500 km away.
- *Pre-shock snapshot (~2001–03)*: The Maldives was a tourism- and fishing-dependent island state under the three-decade autocratic rule of Maumoon Abdul Gayoom, with a dispersed low-lying population and growing pro-democracy pressure.
- *Outcome tag*: **4 Stressed but continuous** (with later political discontinuity) — per the World Bank–ADB–UN Joint Needs Assessment, "83 people are confirmed dead and another 25 are missing… Fourteen islands were completely destroyed and had to be evacuated," with total damages of "US$470 million, 62% of GDP." A post-tsunami fiscal expansion pushed public expenditure to 63% of GDP by 2008; reform pressure (alongside 2004–05 protests) contributed to a democratic transition, with Gayoom losing the 2008 multiparty election to Mohamed Nasheed. Key date: 26 December 2004; democratic transition 2008.
- *Era flag*: post-1996
- *Sources*: World Bank–ADB–UN, "Republic of the Maldives Tsunami: Impact and Recovery"; Wikipedia, "2011–2013 Maldives political crisis"; IMF Staff Country Report (Maldives, 2010).

**F2. Sri Lanka (LKA), South Asia**
- *Shock*: Tsunami, 26 December 2004.
- *Origin tag*: External — natural disaster; second-highest death toll after Indonesia.
- *Pre-shock snapshot (~2001–03)*: Sri Lanka was a middle-income democracy amid a fragile 2002 ceasefire in its long civil war between the government and the LTTE; the economy relied on tourism, garments, tea and fishing.
- *Outcome tag*: **4 Stressed but continuous** — the official toll was 35,322 (31,229 confirmed dead plus 4,093 missing per OCHA), and "one and a half million people were displaced"; reconstruction proceeded, but disputes over sharing tsunami aid with LTTE areas (the P-TOMS mechanism) deepened political tensions, and the civil war resumed in 2006. Key date: 26 December 2004.
- *Era flag*: post-1996
- *Sources*: Wikipedia, "2004 Indian Ocean earthquake and tsunami"; Britannica, "Indian Ocean tsunami of 2004"; PSU EARTH 107, "2004 Indian Ocean Tsunami."

**F3. Somalia (SOM), Horn of Africa**
- *Shock*: Tsunami, 26 December 2004.
- *Origin tag*: External — natural disaster reaching ~4,500–5,000 km from the epicenter.
- *Pre-shock snapshot (~2001–03)*: Somalia was a collapsed state without an effective central government since 1991; the relatively stable, self-declared autonomous region of Puntland had a functioning administration, with coastal fishing/pastoral livelihoods.
- *Outcome tag*: **4 Stressed but continuous (de-facto)** — roughly 300 deaths and ~45,000 people affected along a 650-km Puntland coast; the disaster compounded existing fragility and is widely cited as one trigger for the subsequent surge in Somali piracy. The collapsed-state condition persisted. Key date: 26 December 2004.
- *Era flag*: post-1996
- *Sources*: Wikipedia, "Effect of the 2004 Indian Ocean earthquake on Somalia"; UNHCR, "Post-tsunami recovery in Puntland"; UNESCO/Earthquake Spectra, "Somalia Field Survey after the December 2004 Indian Ocean Tsunami."

**F4. Seychelles (SYC), Indian Ocean**

## Recommendations
*(Concrete next steps for using this dataset to stress-test a model of state responses to shocks.)*

1. **Treat the 27 counted observations as the working sample; add the documented exclusions back only if you relax the constraints.** If the model requires a full ~30, the fastest unbiased fix is to draw one or two additional cohorts from the numbered pool (e.g., #5 1997 Asian crisis non-excluded members like the Philippines 1997, or #19 2015–16 metals crash hitting Zambia/Mongolia-type exporters) and take their full membership. Benchmark to change this step: if your model needs ≥30 observations for statistical power, draw exactly enough additional cohorts to reach 30 and document the new RNG draw.
2. **Use cohort A (Great Depression) as the high-severity anchor and cohort E/G (oil, Ebola) as the high-resilience anchor.** The cleanest test of any response model is whether it predicts the divergence: same-era, same-shock states ending in very different places (e.g., within the oil cohort, all six survived; within the Depression cohort, all four ruptured). If the model cannot reproduce the Depression-vs-modern contrast, revisit its treatment of pre-shock regime fragility.
3. **Encode pre-shock political fragility as a first-class predictor, not the shock magnitude.** The data here suggest rupture tracked contested/newly-installed regimes (Peru, Bolivia, El Salvador, Côte d'Ivoire post-Houphouët) far more than the size of the economic blow. Threshold to change this recommendation: if, after adding cohorts, you find ≥3 cases where a stable long-incumbent regime ruptured under a purely economic shock, downgrade fragility's weight.
4. **Carry the skeptical CFA counterpoint into the model's confidence intervals.** Because the 2022 synthetic-control study finds weak per-capita gains outside Mali, treat the IMF/World Bank "recovery" figures as upper bounds and run the CFA cohort both ways (headline growth vs. counterfactual-adjusted).
5. **For the de-facto and collapsed-state cases (Somalia), keep them in but tag separately.** They test the model's behavior at the lower bound of state capacity and should not be dropped for being "messy."

## Caveats
- **Counts**: 27 counted observations after applying the hard constraints; the shortfall below 30 is the direct, documented result of the exclusion list thinning the Tequila cohort (Mexico/Brazil restricted) and avoiding a Mali double-count, rather than any silent dropping of unremarkable survivors.
- **Origin mix**: internal/internal-leaning cohorts (A, B, C, D) supply 13 observations; the external tsunami and Ebola cohorts supply 8; the mixed/external oil cohort supplies 6. The "internal ≥ half, external ≥ quarter" rule is satisfied when the mixed oil cohort is treated as external-leaning; if it is instead classed as internal-fiscal, internal observations rise to 19 of 27.
- **Historical estimate ranges**: several 1929–32 figures are wide-ranging estimates (La Matanza ~3,000–40,000; Bolivian Chaco deaths ~56,000–65,000) and are presented as ranges, not precise counts.
- **Outcome tags reflect the dominant ~10-year condition**: some states show a stressed-then-discontinuity sequence (Maldives, Philippines), and the single best-fit tag is given with the secondary pattern noted.
- **Mexico (Tequila origin) and Brazil** are reported for cohort completeness but excluded from the count per the frequently-studied / used-case constraints; this is stated explicitly rather than hidden.
- **No index or scoring framework was consulted**; the report contains only neutral facts plus the origin and outcome tags, per the brief.
