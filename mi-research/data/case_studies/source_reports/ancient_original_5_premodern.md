# Stress-Testing the Modernization Index Against Five Pre-Modern Collapses

## TL;DR
- The MI's structural logic travels surprisingly well across 3,000 years: in all five cases the empire that fragmented had a wide pillar spread (a high P2/P4 sitting on a hollowing P1/P5), and fragment counts mostly fell inside the "natural" 2–4 band predicted by surviving institutional capacity — Rome (6–8) and the Mongols' engineered dispersion being the instructive exceptions.
- The single most portable pillar is **P1 (Institutional Quality)**; the framework's central claim that institutional depth predicts both fragmentation pattern and successor-state sophistication is confirmed in 4.5 of 5 cases (Mongol successors inherit the institutions of the *conquered*, not the conquerors — a partial falsification that the framework actually anticipates via "inherited P1").
- The honest verdict: this is a **genuine analytical contribution as a diagnostic checklist, but substantially post-hoc as a predictive instrument**. Ordinal 1–5 scoring of fragmentary ancient evidence is irreducibly subjective, P3 (Human Capital) barely translates, and the framework cannot explain *timing* or the role of contingent exogenous shocks (Inca smallpox, Hunnic disruption) — which is precisely why Safeguard D has to do so much work.

## Key Findings
- **Configuration thesis holds best.** Across all five, durability tracked pillar *balance*, not peak height. The Abbasids had the highest P2 (Innovation) of any contemporary on Earth and still fragmented because P1 never matched it — the clearest ancient analogue to "complexity without institutions."
- **P1 ordinality holds.** Rank empires by pre-collapse P1 and you correctly predict both how many pieces they break into and how sophisticated those pieces are. The Eastern Roman Empire's survival while the West fell is explained almost entirely by a divergence in P1 and P4 (intact eastern tax base) at the pre-collapse time point.
- **The "natural fragmentation range" (2–4) is real but not deterministic.** Han China → 3 (Three Kingdoms); Mongols → 4 khanates; Inca → effectively 1 successor insurgency (Vilcabamba); Abbasids → many (engineered dispersion, low P1); Western Rome → 6–8 (the anomaly, explained by Safeguard D plus collapsed P1).
- **Earned-vs-extracted (P4) applies but needs translation.** "Productive vs extractive" maps onto ancient economies as *broad agrarian-fiscal base vs tribute/single-resource dependence*. Rome's debased denarius, the Abbasids' over-reliance on Sawad/Iraq land tax and trade routes, and the Mongols' pure conquest-tribute model all score as extractive vulnerability (Safeguard E).
- **Safeguard G (suppression vs prevention) is the sharpest cross-temporal tool.** Empires that substituted force for institutional accommodation of new power-holders (Abbasid Turkic *ghilman*, late-Roman *foederati*, Han regional military governors) all decentralized along exactly those fault lines.

## Details

### Framework, method, and epistemics

The Modernization Index assigns five weighted pillars — P1 Institutional Quality (34%), P2 Innovation & Knowledge (15%), P3 Human Capital (16%), P4 Economic Structure & Independence (20%), P5 Stability & Resilience (16%). I score each on a 1–5 ordinal scale **relative to contemporaries**, at peak and pre-collapse, for five empires. I maintain strict phase separation (peak scoring → pre-collapse scoring → predictions → verification) and the six safeguards.

Three epistemic caveats govern everything below. First, ordinal scores on fragmentary ancient data are interpretive; I present alternative scores where evidence is genuinely contested. Second, "relative to contemporaries" is doing heavy lifting — a "5" for the Inca means "best in the pre-Columbian Andes," not "Han-equivalent." Third, the test is of *structural logic*, not numerical precision. I have deliberately erred toward conservative scores and flagged historiographic disputes (e.g., the Harper plague-maximalism debate; the Huayna Capac smallpox-skepticism of Cook and McCaa).

### CASE 1 — LATE WESTERN ROMAN EMPIRE (~350–476 CE)

**Phase 1 — Peak (~100–180 CE, Five Good Emperors).**
- P1 = 5. Professional bureaucracy, codified law, sophisticated census-based taxation, vast administrative reach. Scheidel & Friesen (*Journal of Roman Studies* 99, 2009, pp. 61–91) estimate the empire at its mid-2nd-century demographic peak produced "total output and consumption equivalent to 50 million tons of wheat or close to 20 billion sesterces per year," of which roughly 5% was extracted by the imperial government and élites (~1.5% of the population) controlled about one-fifth of income. Adoptive succession (Nerva–Antonines) temporarily solved the chronic Roman succession problem.
- P2 = 4. High for its era (concrete, aqueducts, codified engineering, literate administration) but innovation was adoption/scaling rather than a knowledge-economy takeoff.
- P3 = 4. Urbanization perhaps the highest in the ancient Mediterranean; deep professional/curial class; Rome itself near a million people.
- P4 = 4. Broad agrarian base, integrated Mediterranean trade, stable high-silver denarius. Marked down from 5 because the economy was substantially slave- and conquest-augmented.
- P5 = 4. The Antonine plague (from 165 CE) "clipped the Roman peace in its bloom" (Harper). Mortality is hotly contested — estimates run from about 2% to 33% of the empire's ~75 million population (1.5–25 million deaths); most syntheses coalesce around ~10% (about 7.5 million), while Harper (*The Fate of Rome*, 2017) estimates worst-affected areas lost ~20%. Pre-165 stability was very high.

**Phase 2 — Pre-collapse (~400–450 CE), scoring BOTH halves.**

*Western half:*
- P1 = 2. Real institutional hollowing. The shift to *foederati* — barbarian groups settled inside the frontier under their own leaders — represents the state outsourcing its core military function and is the single clearest P1-decline indicator. Tax revenue collapsed as the richest provinces (Africa to the Vandals 439, much of Gaul/Spain) left the fiscal system; Heather emphasizes the resulting "deleterious effect upon the field armies of the drastic drop in tax revenues."
- P2 = 2. Ward-Perkins' material-culture evidence (collapse of wheel-thrown pottery, long-distance amphorae, coinage, monumental building, even iron nails and coffins) documents a knowledge/complexity regression underway in the West.
- P3 = 2. De-urbanization, shrinking towns (inhabited area contracting from the 2nd c. onward), decline of literacy.
- P4 = 2. The denarius, decreed at 95–98% silver under Augustus (15 BCE), declined gradually and then precipitously over the next ~270 years to about 2% silver (per L.H. Cope's metallurgical analysis), falling faster in the provinces than in Rome; the western economy was demonetizing and localizing.
- P5 = 1. Endemic usurpation, Hunnic pressure displacing the Goths inward, loss of tax base feeding an army-funding death spiral.

*Eastern half (same date):*
- P1 = 4, P2 = 3, P3 = 3, P4 = 4, P5 = 3. The critical divergence is **P1 and P4**: Constantinople retained a reliable tax base in Egypt, the Levant, and Anatolia, defensible geography, and a larger population (~two-thirds of the whole). The East could absorb battlefield defeats because "gold" kept flowing.

**Phase 3 — Predictions (before verification).** Collapse form: fragmentation of the West into multiple barbarian-military kingdoms; survival of the East. Successor count: with P1 at 2 and a maximum exogenous shock (Safeguard D), the natural 2–4 band should be *exceeded* — predict 5+. Best successor trajectory: whichever retained most Roman institutional scaffolding (predict the Frankish/Gothic kingdoms that preserved Roman law and the Church). Violence: high but uneven (Ward-Perkins vs. the "peaceful transition" school). Primary declining pillar: P1, cascading into P4/P5. Safeguards: D (exogenous Hunnic/Gothic shock) **and** endogenous fiscal-military decay together; G (the *foederati* solution was accommodation-by-necessity that became suppression's failure).

**Phase 4 — Verification.** **Confirmed.** The West fragmented into 6–8 successor kingdoms (Visigoths, Vandals, Ostrogoths, Franks, Burgundians, Sueves, plus Alans/others and Anglo-Saxon Britain) — well above the natural band, exactly as the combination of collapsed P1 and maximal Safeguard D predicts. The East survived 1,000+ years. The Frankish kingdom (best institutional retention, Catholic rather than Arian, co-opting Roman elites) had the best trajectory — confirmed. The genuinely contested element is the *weight* of exogenous shock vs. endogenous decay; Heather stresses external (Hunnic) agency, Ward-Perkins material collapse, Wickham institutional transformation, Harper plague/climate. The framework's verdict — that both operated, but that a hollowed P1/P4 is what turned shock into collapse (the East had the same barbarians on its Danube and survived) — is a defensible synthesis.

### CASE 2 — HAN DYNASTY CHINA (~180–220 CE)

**Phase 1 — Peak (~50–100 CE, early-mid Eastern Han).**
- P1 = 5. Merit-tinged bureaucracy, codified law, commandery-county administration, sophisticated taxation, the most institutionally dense state on Earth. But Lewis stresses a structural flaw baked in from 25 CE: the Eastern Han revival "marked the triumph of locally powerful families over the central court," so administrative reach over great estates was already compromised.
- P2 = 4. Paper, the seismograph, advanced metallurgy, high administrative literacy.
- P3 = 4. Large urbanized population, deep scholar-official class, the imperial academy.
- P4 = 4. Broad agrarian-fiscal base; but Lewis/de Crespigny note the chronic erosion as small farmers commended themselves to tax-exempt magnate estates, shrinking the tax base.
- P5 = 4.

**Phase 2 — Pre-collapse (~180–200 CE).**
- P1 = 2. Capture of the court by eunuch and consort factions; the tax base eroded by magnate estates; in 188 Emperor Ling granted military governors provincial control — the institutionalization of decentralization.
- P2 = 3. Knowledge base intact but state knowledge-institutions decaying.
- P3 = 3.
- P4 = 2. Fiscal crisis: "by 100, bringing in enough money to support the government through taxation was a growing problem" as small farmers exited the tax rolls.
- P5 = 1. The Yellow Turban Rebellion (184 CE), sparked by plague and famine, ~360,000 rebels in three regions; the 189 eunuch massacre; Dong Zhuo's seizure of the capital.

**Phase 3 — Predictions.** Collapse form: warlord fragmentation. Successor count: with P1 ~2 but a *literate centralizing tradition* and no maximal exogenous shock, predict the *low* end of the natural band — 2–4, most likely 3. Best successor: Cao Cao's northern state (Wei), which retained the most bureaucratic infrastructure. Violence: catastrophic demographic loss. Primary declining pillar: P1 (factional capture + military devolution). Safeguards: B (fragment count should match natural range given residual P1); G (the warlord solution was suppression of symptoms — the court used regional militarization to crush the Yellow Turbans and thereby created the warlords).

**Phase 4 — Verification.** **Confirmed.** Three Kingdoms (Wei, Shu, Wu) — exactly 3, dead center of the natural band, and a striking contrast with the 6–8 of post-Roman Europe. The configuration difference is the explanation: Han China retained a *unitary literate-bureaucratic ideal* (the Mandate of Heaven, a common script, a scholar-official class) that capped dispersion and made reunification the default expectation. Wei/Jin did reunify (280 CE) — though the Jin reunification proved fragile and collapsed into the Sixteen Kingdoms, which the framework would read as P1 never having been genuinely rebuilt. The "Chinese cyclical pattern" (fragmentation → reunification) is well explained by persistent high latent P1/P2 even through collapse — the institutional template survived the state.

### CASE 3 — ABBASID CALIPHATE (~850–950 CE)

**Phase 1 — Peak (~780–830 CE, Harun al-Rashid to al-Ma'mun).**
- P1 = 4. Sophisticated *diwan* bureaucracy, vizierate, land-tax (kharaj) administration; but succession was never institutionally solved (the al-Amin/al-Ma'mun civil war of 811–819 is the warning sign).
- P2 = 5. **The highest P2 of any contemporary civilization.** The Graeco-Arabic translation movement (Gutas) systematically rendered nearly all secular Greek science and philosophy into Arabic over the 8th–10th centuries; paper adoption, astronomy, medicine, mathematics. Shatzmiller's wage data show professional skill-premiums peaking in the 9th century, "the Abbasid Golden Age."
- P3 = 4. High urbanization (Baghdad among the world's largest cities), deep professional/scholarly class, rising human capital (Shatzmiller).
- P4 = 4. Vast trade network, sophisticated gold dinar/silver dirham bimetallism, productive agriculture (Sawad of Iraq). Marked down for heavy dependence on Iraqi land-tax and long-distance trade-route revenue (Safeguard E).
- P5 = 3. The unsolved succession and the 811–819 civil war cap this.

**Phase 2 — Pre-collapse (~900–940 CE).**
- P1 = 2. The decisive decline: al-Mu'tasim's creation (from 836) of a Turkic slave-soldier (*ghulam/mamluk*) army, separate from society, culminating in the "Anarchy at Samarra" (861–870) — "a period of extreme internal instability... marked by the violent succession of four caliphs, who became puppets in the hands of powerful rival military groups," beginning with the murder of Caliph al-Mutawakkil by his Turkic guards in December 861. Tax collection lapsed; the caliph became a hostage of his praetorians.
- P2 = 4. Innovation *continued*, but increasingly in autonomous successor courts, not the center — exactly the "scattered innovation on inadequate institutions" pattern.
- P3 = 4.
- P4 = 2. Fiscal collapse of the center; the Sawad tax base degraded; revenue captured by regional governors (Tulunids in Egypt, etc.).
- P5 = 1. Provincial secession, the Zanj revolt (869–883), the Qarmatians, and military praetorianism.

**Phase 3 — Predictions.** Collapse form: not a single clean break but progressive *de facto* fragmentation into many autonomous emirates under nominal caliphal sovereignty. Successor count: with P1 at 2 and high P2/P4 — the "complexity without institutions" signature — predict **engineered dispersion, 5+** (Safeguard B violated). Best successors: those inheriting the high P2 (Buyid Iraq/Iran, Fatimid Egypt, later Andalus) should preserve and extend the science. Violence: chronic but lower-intensity than Rome. Primary declining pillar: P1 (military praetorianism), with P5 cascading. Safeguards: E (rentier dependence on Iraqi land-tax + trade routes); G (the *ghulam* system was the purest suppression-not-prevention failure — buying loyalty with force instead of institutionalizing the new military class).

**Phase 4 — Verification.** **Confirmed, and the strongest single vindication of the configuration thesis.** The caliphate fractured into many entities (Tulunids/Ikhshidids in Egypt, Buyids who took Baghdad itself in 945, Samanids, Hamdanids, Fatimids, Umayyad Córdoba) — far above the natural band, the predicted engineered dispersion. Crucially, **innovation survived in the successor states**: Kennedy's "paradox that the first Muslim empire was broken up by the success of Islam" — local Muslim elites no longer needed the center. The high-P2/low-P1 configuration produced exactly what the framework predicts: a civilization that stayed brilliant while its state dissolved. The modern-Middle-East parallel (resource/rentier wealth and high human capital on weak central institutions producing fragmentation) is structurally apt, though it should be offered as analogy, not law.

### CASE 4 — MONGOL EMPIRE (~1227–1294)

**Phase 1 — Peak (~1240–1260).**
- P1 = 2. The purest test case: **maximum territory, minimum institutions.** The *yam* relay-postal system, the *yassa* law-code, and Uyghur-script record-keeping were real but thin; administration was largely borrowed from conquered Chinese, Persian, and Muslim bureaucrats (Ögedei's civilian government placed local bureaucracies under a central overseer). Succession was catastrophically unsolved — the *kurultai* election system invited civil war.
- P2 = 2 (indigenous) / high (appropriated). The Mongols were superb adopters/transmitters of others' technology (siege engineering, the Pax Mongolica's knowledge transfer) but originated little; score reflects native institutions.
- P3 = 2.
- P4 = 1. **Pure conquest-tribute economy** — the textbook Safeguard E extraction model, with negligible productive base of its own.
- P5 = 2. Held together only while the *kurultai* could agree on a khagan.

**Phase 2 — Pre-collapse (~1260–1270).**
- P1 = 2 (unchanged — never built). P4 = 1. P5 = 1: Möngke's death in 1259 with no declared successor triggered the Toluid Civil War (1260–1264, Kublai vs. Ariq Böke), the Berke–Hulegu war (1261–62), and the Kaidu–Kublai war.

**Phase 3 — Predictions.** Collapse form: **rapid fragmentation** — the framework's clearest prediction, because P1 was never built to match the conquered territory. Successor count: 4 (the appanage structure of Genghis's four sons channels dispersion into the natural band *despite* low P1 — an interesting case where inherited structure substitutes for institutions). Best successor trajectory: **whichever inherited the most sophisticated conquered institutions** — predict the Yuan (Chinese bureaucracy) and Ilkhanate (Persian administration) to outshine the Golden Horde (thinner Russian/steppe base) and Chagatai (steppe heartland). Violence: high. Primary declining pillar: P1 (or rather, its permanent absence). Safeguards: D minimal (this was endogenous succession failure, not external conquest); E maximal (tribute economy); G (rule by suppression, no institutional accommodation of the conquered).

**Phase 4 — Verification.** **Confirmed, including the subtle prediction.** Four khanates (Yuan, Golden Horde, Chagatai, Ilkhanate) — in the natural band, but for a reason the framework names explicitly: the *inherited* appanage structure, not Mongol institution-building, supplied the dividing lines. The **inherited-P1 prediction is the headline confirmation**: the Yuan adopted Chinese bureaucracy, era names, and silver-backed paper money; the Ilkhanate ran a Persian "Tajik" administration and sparked the "Ilkhanid Renaissance." Both vastly outclassed the Golden Horde and Chagatai in administrative sophistication — exactly because successor sophistication tracked the P1 of the *conquered substrate*, not the conquerors. This is a *partial falsification-turned-confirmation*: it falsifies any reading of P1 as a property of the ruling people, but confirms the deeper claim that institutional substrate predicts successor trajectory. The framework's note that the Ilkhanate's silver-paper-money experiment (1294) failed where the Yuan's succeeded is a neat P1/P4-execution datapoint.

### CASE 5 — INCA EMPIRE (~1438–1533)

**Phase 1 — Peak (~1490–1520).**
- P1 = 5 (by regional standards). The most centralized, territorial state in the pre-Columbian Americas: decimal census administration, the *mit'a* labor tax, *mitmaq* forced resettlement, state storehouses (*qollqa*), the ~40,000 km Qhapaq Ñan road network (UNESCO's conservatively-mapped figure is ~30,000 km), khipu record-keeping — D'Altroy notes administrative sophistication that "rivalled that of contemporaneous literate states." **But succession was never institutionalized** (no primogeniture rule; contested successions were endemic — Rostworowski's point).
- P2 = 3. Extraordinary engineering and administration, but a hard ceiling: **no writing and no true market/money economy.** Khipu encoded numbers and some narrative but were not a general writing system. This caps P2 and P3 structurally.
- P3 = 3. High organization and life-support (storehouses buffered famine), but no education system in the literate sense; the no-writing ceiling.
- P4 = 3. A non-monetary mobilization economy of remarkable reach but limited diversification; "productive" in agrarian terms (terracing, freeze-drying) but extractive in its labor-tribute logic.
- P5 = 4 (at true peak, under Huayna Capac before ~1525).

**Phase 2 — Pre-collapse (~1527–1532).**
- P1 = 4 (administrative machinery intact but leaderless after the decapitation).
- P5 = **1 — catastrophic.** This is the entire story. Huayna Capac and his heir Ninan Cuyochi died ~1527 (probably, but contestedly, of smallpox — Cook and McCaa urge skepticism, noting thin documentary evidence for a pre-1530 Andean epidemic). The succession war between Huáscar (Cusco) and Atahualpa (Quito), 1529–1532, ended with Atahualpa's victory at Quipaipán just months before Pizarro landed.

**Phase 3 — Predictions.** Collapse form: this is where the framework must explain how 168 men under Pizarro toppled the largest pre-Columbian empire. The answer should be **in the configuration**: high P1 (a centralized state vulnerable to decapitation) combined with a P5 that had cratered to 1, plus Safeguard D at maximum (an utterly exogenous biological+military shock the system had no template for). Prediction: *without* the civil war (P5 intact), the same P1 could have resisted — the Inca state was not institutionally rotten, it was decapitated mid-convulsion. Successor count: ~1 (the neo-Inca Vilcabamba rump), because high P1 + centralization resists *dispersion* even as it succumbs to *decapitation*. Best successor: Vilcabamba (1537–1572). Violence: extreme but compressed. Primary declining pillar: P5 (with P2/P3 structurally capped throughout). Safeguards: D at maximum; B (high P1 predicts *low* fragment count — confirmed by the single rump state).

**Phase 4 — Verification.** **Confirmed.** The centralized state was decapitated by Atahualpa's capture at the Battle of Cajamarca (16 November 1532) — Pizarro fielded just 168 Spaniards, "106 on foot and 62 on horseback," against an Inca force estimated at roughly 80,000. With the bureaucracy and two armies intact but "no one to give orders" (MacQuarrie), the Spanish marched to Cusco. Only the neo-Inca state of Vilcabamba persisted (to 1572) — fragment count ≈ 1, exactly as high P1 predicts. The **counterfactual is the framework's strongest insight**, and the comparison with the Aztec collapse is diagnostic. The Aztec empire was *hegemonic/tributary* (Hassig's territorial-vs-hegemonic distinction, *Aztec Warfare* 1988; Smith & Berdan, "Archaeology and the Aztec Empire," *World Archaeology* 23, 1992): it ruled indirectly, left ~40–50 autonomous altepetl paying scheduled tribute, built no provincial bureaucracy or roads — and so bred *defection*. Cortés weaponized a coalition of resentful tributaries and the never-conquered Tlaxcala: per Matthew Restall (*Seven Myths of the Spanish Conquest*, OUP), he first reached Tenochtitlán with "6,000 of these allies and only 250 Spaniards," and "the final assault on the capital was carried out with 200,000 native allies." The Inca's *territorial/centralized* structure bred the opposite vulnerability — *decapitation* rather than defection. **Two structures, two distinct failure modes, both correctly readable from the pillar configuration**: low-P1 hegemony fails by coalition defection; high-P1 centralization fails by decapitation when P5 has collapsed. The no-writing P2/P3 ceiling is the one place the framework's "relative to contemporaries" rule strains — relative to the Andes the Inca were a 5 on P1, but the absence of writing imposed a hard complexity ceiling that mattered at contact.

## Final Synthesis

**1. Overall scorecard (pre-collapse, weighted).** Ranked by pre-collapse weighted MI: Eastern Rome (highest, survived) > Han China ≈ Abbasid center > Inca (high P1, cratered P5) > Western Rome ≈ Mongol core (lowest). Survival/fragmentation tracks the score and, more importantly, the *spread*.

**2. Does P1 ordinality hold?** **Yes, robustly.** In every case the pre-collapse P1 score predicts both fragment count and successor sophistication. The Mongol case appears to violate it (low P1, sophisticated successors) but actually confirms the deeper "inherited P1" mechanism: successor sophistication tracked the institutions of the *conquered*, not the conquerors.

**3. Does the natural fragmentation range (2–4) hold?** **Partially — and the exceptions are the most informative data.** Han (3), Mongol (4), Inca (~1) fall in or below the band. Western Rome (6–8) and the Abbasids (many) *exceed* it — and in both, the framework correctly flags why: collapsed P1 (Safeguard B "engineered dispersion") plus, for Rome, maximal exogenous shock (Safeguard D). So the range is not a law but a conditional prediction keyed to P1.

**4. Does the configuration thesis (balanced = durable, wide spread = vulnerable) hold?** **This is the framework's best result.** Durability tracked balance, not peak height. The Abbasids (P2=5 on P1=2) and Mongols (vast conquest on P1=2) are the clearest "wide spread = vulnerable" cases. Eastern Rome's relative balance explains its survival. The thesis is confirmed in all five.

**5. Does earned-vs-extracted (P4) apply to ancient economies?** **Yes, with translation.** "Productive vs extractive" becomes "broad agrarian-fiscal base vs tribute/single-resource/trade-route dependence." Mongol tribute (E maximal), Abbasid Iraqi-land-tax + trade-route dependence, Roman debasement, and Inca labor-tribute all read as extractive vulnerability. The one strain: the Inca had no money or markets at all, so "monetary system sophistication" must be reinterpreted as "fiscal-mobilization sophistication."

**6. Which pillar translates best/worst?** **Best: P1** (institutions are recognizable across millennia). **Worst: P3** (Human Capital). Life-expectancy and "education system" proxies are nearly unmeasurable for these societies and pre-modern life expectancy varied little across contemporaries; P3 added the least discriminating signal. P2 translates only with an "indigenous vs appropriated" caveat (Mongols) and a "non-literate ceiling" caveat (Inca).

**7. Does suppression-vs-prevention (Safeguard G) apply?** **Yes — it may be the most portable single tool.** Every case features a ruling order substituting force for the institutional accommodation of a rising power-holder: Abbasid Turkic *ghilman*, Roman *foederati*, Han regional military governors, Mongol rule-by-conquest, Inca decapitation-vulnerable autocracy. Decentralization consistently followed those exact fault lines.

**8. What does the framework explain that existing explanations miss?** It reframes the Rome East/West divergence as a *measurable P1/P4 gap at a single time point* rather than a narrative of "decline." It identifies the Abbasid paradox (brilliance amid dissolution) as a structural configuration, not a cultural mystery. And it unifies the Aztec and Inca collapses as two failure modes of one variable (P1 structure) rather than two unrelated stories.

**9. What does the framework FAIL to explain?** **Timing and contingency.** It cannot tell you *when* collapse comes or predict the specific exogenous trigger. The Inca smallpox shock, the Hunnic displacement of the Goths, and the precise dating of the Antonine plague are all contingencies the framework can only absorb retroactively via Safeguard D. It also cannot weigh climate (Harper's cooling/aridification) except as a generic P5 stressor. And it says little about *ideological* cohesion (the Mandate of Heaven, caliphal religious legitimacy, the Roman idea) that demonstrably shaped reunification odds.

**10. Does complexity-capacity matching generalize across 3,000 years?** **Yes, as a qualitative principle; no, as a quantitative law.** The core insight — that complexity (territory, population, economic/innovative sophistication) must be matched by institutional capacity (P1) or the surplus complexity sheds via fragmentation — recurs in all five. But the matching is ordinal and directional, not metric: I cannot defend the specific weights (34/15/16/20/16) against ancient data, only the rank-ordering they imply.

**11. Honest assessment: genuine contribution or post-hoc pattern-matching?** **Both, and intellectual honesty requires saying so.** As a *predictive* instrument applied blind, the framework is weak: the ordinal scores are interpreter-dependent, the phase-3 "predictions" are made by someone who already knows the outcomes, and several confirmations (especially fragment counts) are loose enough to accommodate a range of results. As a *diagnostic and comparative* instrument, it is genuinely valuable: it forces explicit, decomposable, falsifiable claims; it correctly isolates P1 and configuration-balance as the load-bearing variables; and it generates the framework's two best original insights (Rome's East/West divergence as a P1/P4 gap; Aztec-vs-Inca as two failure modes of one structural variable). The five carefully-hedged verdicts above are worth more than five triumphant confirmations would be — and the framework's own safeguards (B, D, E, G) are what keep it from overfitting. **Verdict: a strong heuristic, a weak oracle.**

## Recommendations
- **Use the MI as a comparative diagnostic, not a forecasting oracle.** Its decision-value is in forcing explicit, decomposable claims about *where* an institutional gap sits, not in predicting collapse dates. Treat any fragment-count or timing "prediction" as a low-confidence band.
- **Weight P1 and the configuration-spread metric above the others** for pre-modern application; consider folding P3 into P1/P4 because its proxies are too weak to discriminate across contemporaries.
- **Operationalize the safeguards as the primary analytical engine.** Safeguard G (suppression vs. prevention) and Safeguard B (P1-keyed fragment-count expectation) carried more explanatory weight than the raw pillar scores. Make them first-class, not addenda.
- **Add an explicit exogenous-shock and ideology module.** The framework's biggest blind spots — contingent triggers (Safeguard D doing too much work) and legitimating ideology (Mandate of Heaven, caliphate, Romanitas) — should become scored inputs rather than narrative escape hatches.
- **Benchmark that would change these conclusions:** if applied *blind* (scorer ignorant of outcomes) to a held-out set of pre-modern cases the framework predicted fragment counts within ±1 and primary-declining-pillar correctly in ≥70% of cases, it would graduate from heuristic to weak predictor. Absent that test, treat all confirmations here as in-sample.

## Caveats
- Ordinal 1–5 scoring of fragmentary, contested ancient evidence is irreducibly interpretive; alternative scorings are defensible and I have flagged the live disputes (Harper plague-maximalism vs. Huebner/Newfield/Mordechai; Cook/McCaa smallpox-skepticism; Heather/Ward-Perkins/Wickham/Harper on Rome's causes).
- Phase-3 "predictions" were generated with knowledge of outcomes; they test the framework's *internal coherence*, not its blind predictive power.
- "Relative to contemporaries" makes cross-case score comparison hazardous: an Inca P1 of 5 (Andean-relative) is not a Han P1 of 5 (Old-World-relative).
- Several quantitative anchors are themselves contested in the literature (Antonine plague mortality ~2–33%; Andean depopulation 50–90%; Qhapaq Ñan length 30,000–40,000 km; Roman GDP estimates) and are presented as ranges, not points.
- Many supporting figures were retrieved via reviews and syntheses rather than the primary monographs (Hassig, D'Altroy page-level citations in particular); verbatim quotations should be confirmed against first editions before formal publication.
