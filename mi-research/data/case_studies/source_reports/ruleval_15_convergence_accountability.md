# Testing the Convergence Qualifier and the Accountability Gap: 15 Case Studies

## TL;DR
- **Pattern 1 (Convergence Qualifier) holds directionally but is not deterministic:** a widening gap with institutions falling (Greece, Nicaragua) reliably preceded crisis, and high institutions buffered pure economic shocks (Ireland, Botswana) — but resource-rich, low-institution states absorbed enormous widening gaps for decades without collapse (Equatorial Guinea, Gabon), and a "closing gap" was no protection when the closing was a donor-financed façade (Mali). Direction matters, but *what is driving* the convergence matters more.
- **Pattern 2 (Accountability Gap) is the weakest-supported of the three:** while Iran 1979 and Romania 1989 are textbook succession/legitimacy collapses, the disconfirming cases are strong and numerous — Oman (2020), China (2002–2012), and Cuba (2006–2021) all executed leadership transitions without any accountability mechanism. High-capacity unaccountable states fail this way *sometimes*, but institutionalized succession rules substitute for accountability more often than the hypothesis allows.
- **The democratic-drift gap is real and under-documented:** genuine sustained institutional decline from a top-tier base is historically rare (Israel is nearly unique and still unfolding), and the two closest precedents (Iceland 2008, Italy 1992) both reconstituted rather than collapsed — suggesting top-tier democracies have reservoirs of self-correction the MI's lower-base crisis cases lacked. The thinness of this evidence is itself the finding.

## Key Findings

1. **Direction of the gap is a real signal, but the *mechanism* driving institutional change is the deeper variable.** The protective/dangerous distinction the pattern proposes survives contact with the evidence, but several cases show that "rising" or "falling" institutional scores can be misleading proxies for the underlying state.

2. **The strongest disconfirmation of Pattern 1 comes from petro-authoritarian durability.** Equatorial Guinea and Gabon ran extreme, decades-long P4–P1 gaps (high income, near-bottom institutions) without the predicted crisis. Oil rents financed repression and patronage that substituted for institutional quality. This is the clearest "widening gap absorbed without crisis" evidence.

3. **The "closing gap reversed" scenario is best illustrated by Mali**, where two decades of internationally-celebrated democratic consolidation (1992–2012) proved hollow and collapsed in months — showing that measured institutional gains can be a "donor darling" artifact rather than real convergence.

4. **Pattern 2's disconfirming cases are more numerous than its confirming ones.** Three of five high-capacity authoritarian cases managed succession without accountability mechanisms. The hypothesis needs a qualifier: the danger is not the *absence of accountability* per se, but the *absence of any institutionalized succession rule* (whether accountable or not).

5. **Top-tier democratic erosion is evidentially thin because it is genuinely rare.** This corroborates the premise behind the coverage gap. Israel is the one clear contemporary case of measured decline from the top tier; historical near-analogs tended to absorb shocks and reconstitute.

## Details

### PATTERN 1 — THE CONVERGENCE QUALIFIER (7 cases)

---

**CASE 1 — Greece (2001–2010) — Pattern 1**
*Side of pattern: CONFIRMS (widening gap, institutions lagging income → crisis).*

- **Structural situation:** After euro entry (2001), Greek GDP per capita converged rapidly toward the EU average on cheap credit, but governance, public administration, statistical integrity, pension sustainability and competitiveness lagged badly. The Bank for International Settlements review by Yannis Stournaras notes Maastricht convergence was "nominal" — it did not require "deep and sustainable reforms in...public administration." Greece displayed "a big asymmetry in institutional quality...relative to its EU partners." This is a textbook widening P4–P1 gap (income up, institutions flat/eroding relative to peers).
- **What happened:** In 2009–2010, the sudden recognition of fiscal and institutional imbalances (including falsified statistics) reversed confidence; GDP collapsed, debt-to-GDP exploded, and Greece was shut out of capital markets. It received €288.7bn in rescue loans across three bailouts (2010, 2012, 2015) — per the European Stability Mechanism, "Greece has received a total of €288.7 billion in rescue loans since 2010" — and became the only eurozone member never to cleanly exit its adjustment programme.
- **Data availability:** WGI-era; very rich. WGI governance indicators, Eurostat/IMF data, extensive scholarship.

---

**CASE 2 — Botswana (1966–present) — Pattern 1**
*Side of pattern: CONFIRMS (institutions high relative to income; gap closing/managed → no crisis).*

- **Structural situation:** Botswana built strong institutions (rule of law, property rights, parliamentary oversight, independent judiciary, low corruption) *ahead of or alongside* its diamond-driven income growth — averaging ~9% real GDP growth 1966–2006. Institutions kept pace with or exceeded what its income level would predict. This is the protective configuration: a manageable gap with P1 high/rising.
- **What happened:** No systemic crisis across nearly six decades; longest continuous multiparty democracy in Africa; moved from one of the world's poorest countries to upper-middle-income. Notably, in 2024 Botswana experienced its first-ever peaceful transfer of power away from the long-ruling BDP — a sign of institutional health, not crisis. (Caveat: V-Dem recorded democratic *erosion* 2010–2023, stripping liberal-democracy status in 2021 before a 2024–25 rebound; but no economic-institutional crisis of the MI type ever materialized.)
- **Data availability:** WGI-era strong; pre-1996 requires Polity and historical scholarship (well covered).

---

**CASE 3 — Equatorial Guinea (1996–present) — Pattern 1**
*Side of pattern: WEAKENS (extreme widening gap absorbed without crisis).*

- **Structural situation:** Post-1996 offshore oil discoveries made Equatorial Guinea the richest country in Africa per capita, while institutions remained among the world's weakest (near-bottom WGI on rule of law, voice, control of corruption; oil revenues a declared "state secret"). This is a maximal widening P4–P1 gap — high P4, floor-level P1 — sustained for ~30 years.
- **What happened:** No regime crisis. Obiang, in power since 1979, remains the world's longest-serving president; in the 2022 elections he was re-elected for a sixth term with 94.9% of the vote on a reported 98% turnout, and the ruling PDGE coalition swept all 100 Chamber and 55 elected Senate seats. Despite roughly 12 real/alleged coup attempts, oil rents financed a repressive apparatus and patronage network that absorbed the gap. The predicted crisis simply did not arrive.
- **Why it weakens the pattern:** The "large gap predicts crisis" core fails here, and the directional qualifier does not rescue it — institutions never rose, yet no crisis. Rents are the omitted variable.
- **Data availability:** WGI-era; rich (HRW, ICIJ, academic political-economy literature).

---

**CASE 4 — Mali (1992–2012) — Pattern 1**
*Side of pattern: WEAKENS / COMPLICATES (closing gap that reversed and ended in crisis).*

- **Structural situation:** From 1992 Mali was the "donor darling" and "poster child for democracy" — measured institutional indicators (elections, peaceful alternation in 2002) suggested institutions catching up to (low) income: a closing gap. But beneath the façade, corruption was endemic and state capacity in the north was hollow.
- **What happened:** In 2012 a Tuareg insurgency, a junior-officer coup, and a jihadist takeover of the north collapsed the state within months — what one analysis called Mali's "institutional nudity." This is precisely a "closing gap reversed" case: the catch-up was an aid-financed artifact, and when it stopped, collapse followed. Three coups since 2012.
- **Why it weakens the pattern:** A closing gap was supposed to be protective; here it provided no protection because the convergence was not real. This is the most important Pattern 1 disconfirmation on the "closing gap" side.
- **Data availability:** WGI-era; strong (Bergamaschi, Brookings, Clingendael, Afrobarometer).

---

**CASE 5 — Ireland (2000–2013) — Pattern 1**
*Side of pattern: CONFIRMS (high institutions buffer an economic shock — direction/level of P1 protective).*

- **Structural situation:** Ireland entered the 2008 crisis with strong institutions (high WGI rule of law, control of corruption) but a massive property/banking bubble. Per the Irish banking inquiry, the six main banks' international bond borrowings "grew from less than €16 billion in 2003 to approximately €100 billion (well over half of Ireland's GDP) by 2007." When the bubble burst, the economic shock was severe.
- **What happened:** Ireland implemented a €64bn bank bailout and entered an €85bn EU/IMF programme agreed in December 2010. Per the European Commission, the package "was put in place primarily to bail out the Irish banks… Ireland successfully exited the three year programme on 15 December 2013." Unlike Greece, Ireland absorbed the shock without institutional collapse — orderly government change, credible adjustment, strong recovery thereafter. High P1 converted a potential institutional crisis into a (painful) economic correction.
- **Why it confirms:** Demonstrates the protective value of high institutional quality when an economic shock would otherwise widen the gap — the mirror image of Greece, with similar shock but different institutional base and different outcome.
- **Data availability:** WGI-era; very rich.

---

**CASE 6 — Gabon (1996–2023) — Pattern 1**
*Side of pattern: COMPLICATES (extreme widening gap absorbed for ~27 years, then succession-linked coup).*

- **Structural situation:** Like Equatorial Guinea, an OPEC petro-state with one of Africa's highest per-capita GDPs (~$8,820) but weak institutions, entrenched corruption, and 56 years of single-family (Bongo) rule. A sustained large widening gap.
- **What happened:** The gap was absorbed for decades — but the 2023 military coup (following Ali Bongo's 2018 stroke, a foiled 2019 coup, and a disputed 2023 election) eventually ended the dynasty. Crucially, the trigger was succession/legitimacy (a dynastic palace coup by a Bongo relative, Brice Oligui Nguema), not economic-institutional erosion.
- **Why it complicates:** Supports the "gap eventually matters" intuition but on a multi-decade lag that defeats the predictive value, and the actual mechanism was succession (Pattern 2 territory), not the durability gap. Illustrates the patterns overlapping.
- **Data availability:** WGI-era; good.

---

**CASE 7 — Nicaragua (2007–2018+) — Pattern 1**
*Side of pattern: CONFIRMS (widening gap with P1 FALLING while economy held → crisis).*

- **Structural situation:** Under Ortega from 2007, Nicaragua delivered sustained economic growth and low crime (P4 stable/rising) while systematically dismantling institutional checks — courts, electoral bodies, the police were captured one by one (P1 clearly falling). This is the dangerous configuration the pattern flags: institutions eroding while income holds.
- **What happened:** The April 2018 protests (sparked by social-security reform) met lethal repression — the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights documented "at least 355 people dead, more than 2,000 injured" between April 2018 and July 2019 — and the regime consolidated into outright electoral authoritarianism (2021 election with opposition jailed). A legitimacy crisis erupted precisely as institutional erosion peaked.
- **Why it confirms:** Direct support for "large gap with P1 falling = dangerous," and it links Patterns 1 and 2 — the falling-institutions trajectory produced a succession/legitimacy-style rupture.
- **Data availability:** WGI-era; rich (V-Dem, Crisis Group, Cambridge LAPS).

---

### PATTERN 2 — THE ACCOUNTABILITY GAP (5 cases)

---

**CASE 8 — Iran under the Shah (c.1963–1979) — Pattern 2**
*Side of pattern: CONFIRMS (high-capacity, oil-rich, unaccountable state → sudden legitimacy collapse).*

- **Structural situation:** By the 1970s Pahlavi Iran was a high-capacity modernizing state: enormous oil wealth, a formidable military, rapid growth, technocratic administration — but with no accountability channel (SAVAK repression, no real electoral correction, clergy and bazaar alienated by the White Revolution). The state "looked like a monumental success" externally.
- **What happened:** When grievances (inflation, inequality, repression, cultural alienation) needed an outlet, there was none. The regime collapsed with stunning speed in 1978–79 — "a house of cards." This is the archetypal Pattern 2 failure mode: rich, capable, unaccountable, and unable to self-correct when it needed to.
- **Data availability:** Pre-WGI; requires Polity, historical scholarship (well documented).

---

**CASE 9 — Romania under Ceaușescu (1965–1989) — Pattern 2**
*Side of pattern: CONFIRMS (high-capacity unaccountable Stalinist state → sudden collapse and violent succession).*

- **Structural situation:** A high-penetration totalitarian state (pervasive Securitate, debt fully repaid by March 1989 through brutal austerity) with zero accountability and a personality cult. State capacity was high; legitimacy and self-correction capacity were nil.
- **What happened:** Collapse came in days in December 1989: a Timișoara protest spread, Ceaușescu was shouted down at a televised rally, the army withdrew support, and he and Elena were executed on Christmas Day. The absence of any peaceful course-correction channel turned an ordinary protest into total regime collapse.
- **Nuance:** Scholarship (Monash/Przeworski-model analysis) shows the transition was partly an elite-led "kidnapped revolution" — second-tier nomenklatura captured it. This complicates a pure "popular legitimacy collapse" reading.
- **Data availability:** Pre-WGI; rich historical scholarship.

---

**CASE 10 — Oman (2020 succession) — Pattern 2**
*Side of pattern: WEAKENS (high-capacity unaccountable monarchy → smooth succession, no crisis).*

- **Structural situation:** Sultan Qaboos ruled absolutely for 50 years, holding every key portfolio (head of state, PM, defense, foreign affairs, finance), with only a consultative Majlis — i.e., no genuine accountability mechanism. Widespread fear that his death would bring instability, given no groomed successor and a "murky" process.
- **What happened:** Per the Carnegie Endowment, Qaboos died January 10, 2020 and Haitham bin Tariq was sworn in within hours on January 11 in "one of the most transparent and orderly succession processes in modern Arab history"; the Defence Council opened the sealed letter on state TV under the 1996 Basic Law's three-day rule. The transition was swift, orderly, and stable. An *institutionalized succession rule* substituted for accountability.
- **Why it weakens:** Directly contradicts "unaccountable high-capacity states can't self-correct at succession." A pre-committed succession rule did the job. (Caveat: this tests *succession*, not a *legitimacy* crisis; the regime has not yet faced a Qaboos-scale popular grievance shock.)
- **Data availability:** WGI-era; good (Carnegie, Duck of Minerva, regional analysis).

---

**CASE 11 — China (1978–2012 institutionalized succession; Xi reversal) — Pattern 2**
*Side of pattern: WEAKENS, then COMPLICATES.*

- **Structural situation:** Post-Mao, Deng engineered fixed terms, term limits, mandatory retirement ages, and collective leadership — an attempt to solve authoritarianism's "Achilles heel" (succession) *without* accountability. The Jiang→Hu (2002) and Hu→Xi (2012) transitions were the first orderly premortem successions in PRC history.
- **What happened:** A very high-capacity unaccountable state managed two leadership transitions without crisis — strong disconfirmation of Pattern 2 — by substituting intra-party institutionalization for accountability.
- **The complication:** Under Xi (term limits abolished 2018; return to personalistic rule), China has *dismantled* exactly those succession norms. The MI-relevant prediction is forward-looking: Xi has recreated the unresolved-succession vulnerability the 1980s–2000s reforms had patched. So China both weakens the pattern (1978–2012) and may yet confirm it (post-2018) — a powerful illustration that the relevant variable is *institutionalized succession*, not accountability.
- **Data availability:** WGI-era; rich (Journal of Democracy, China Leadership Monitor, academic literature).

---

**CASE 12 — Cuba (2006–2021 succession) — Pattern 2**
*Side of pattern: WEAKENS (high state-penetration unaccountable one-party state → managed multi-step succession).*

- **Structural situation:** A high-capacity one-party state (deep social penetration, strong security/military-economic apparatus via GAESA) with no accountability mechanism. Faced the ultimate test: replacing the founding charismatic leadership.
- **What happened:** Fidel→Raúl (2006/2008), then Raúl→Díaz-Canel (president 2018, party first secretary 2021) — the first non-Castro leader since 1959 — was executed as a carefully managed, stability-preserving party succession. No regime collapse despite severe economic crisis and the 2021 protests.
- **Why it weakens:** Another unaccountable high-capacity state self-correcting (at the leadership level) via party institutionalization. (Caveat: as of 2026 Cuba faces acute economic/legitimacy stress and external pressure; the succession held, but the *legitimacy* test is ongoing — so this weakens the succession claim more cleanly than the legitimacy claim.)
- **Data availability:** WGI-era; good, though regime opacity limits some structural assessment.

---

### COVERAGE GAP — TOP-TIER DEMOCRATIC DRIFT (3 cases)

---

**CASE 13 — Israel (2017–2025) — Drift gap**
*Side: The clearest contemporary case of sustained measured decline from a top-tier base; outcome still open (eroding, not yet stabilized or in crisis).*

- **Structural situation:** Israel entered this period a top-tier democracy (V-Dem had classed it a liberal democracy since 1973). It then experienced multi-year erosion centered on the 2023 judicial overhaul (executive aggrandizement, attacks on judicial independence, weakened gatekeepers), compounded by polarization, repeated electoral deadlock (five elections 2019–2022), and the post-Oct-2023 war context.
- **What happened / trajectory:** The V-Dem 2024 Democracy Report states: "Israel lost its long-time status as a liberal democracy in 2023. It is now classified as an electoral democracy – for the first time in over 50 years… primarily due to substantial declines in the indicators measuring the transparency and predictability of the law, and government attacks on the judiciary." V-Dem's Liberal Democracy Index fell from 0.656 (2022) to 0.634 (2023), down from a 2000 peak of 0.701. Freedom House scored Israel 80/100 in 2017, 74 in 2024, and 73 in 2025 (still "Free"). World Bank WGI Rule of Law dropped sharply from an estimate of 0.94 (2022) to 0.78 (2023) — its lowest in the 1996–2023 series — and Voice & Accountability slipped from 0.68 (2022) to 0.64 (2023), down from a 2016 peak of 0.79. Scholars (Gidron, Roznai) describe the erosion continuing in "small increments." No stabilization or reversal yet established; this is the live analog to the US concern.
- **Why it matters for the gap:** This is the dataset's missing archetype — a top-15-20% democracy in sustained, measured institutional decline. It demonstrates the phenomenon is possible and is occurring, but does not yet show the endpoint.
- **Data availability:** WGI-era; very rich (V-Dem, Freedom House, WGI, large scholarly literature). Caveats: V-Dem recalculates its full back-series annually (values drift ±0.01–0.02 between versions); V-Dem scores are computed within the Green Line and exclude governance of Palestinians in occupied territories, a contested methodological boundary; some secondary sources misdate the reclassification to 2024 (the authoritative data-year is 2023, reported March 2024).

---

**CASE 14 — Iceland (2008–2013) — Drift gap**
*Side: COMPLICATES/REASSURES (top-tier democracy, measurable dip from a high base, then recovery — high base proved resilient).*

- **Structural situation:** Pre-2008 Iceland was unambiguously top-tier — ranked #1 on the UN Human Development Index (2008) and a perfect-100 Freedom House democracy, with bank assets ~9x GDP. The 2008 banking collapse (largest relative to GDP in history) was simultaneously an economic and a legitimacy/trust shock.
- **What happened / trajectory:** The shock produced the "Pots and Pans Revolution," a government resignation (January 2009), a special investigative commission, prosecutions of bankers, and an (ultimately shelved) constituent-assembly constitution. Freedom House's score slipped from 100 toward 95 ("fraying social capital," per Gylfason). But institutions absorbed the shock and the country recovered strongly by 2012–2013.
- **Why it matters:** A top-tier democracy *can* register measurable institutional erosion under stress — but here the high base provided self-correction (peaceful transition, accountability via prosecutions) and the decline was modest and partly reversed. This is the optimistic counter-scenario for the US concern.
- **Data availability:** WGI-era; rich (Freedom House, NBER, BIS, Britannica).

---

**CASE 15 — Italy, First Republic collapse (1992–1994) — Drift gap**
*Side: COMPLICATES (wealthy established democracy, sharp institutional rupture, reconstituted rather than collapsed; but base was not truly top-tier on corruption).*

- **Structural situation:** Italy was a G7 democracy with free press and competitive elections, but with endemic, systemic corruption (so its institutional base was high on some dimensions, compromised on others). The Tangentopoli/Mani Pulite investigations (1992–94) exposed corruption "institutionally tolerated and part of how the system worked."
- **What happened / trajectory:** The entire First Republic party system collapsed — the Christian Democrats and Socialists dissolved, a technocratic government formed (Ciampi, 1993), electoral law was rewritten by referendum, and a "Second Republic" emerged. Crucially, this was *reconstitution within constitutional bounds*, not regime collapse — but corruption resumed and trust never fully recovered (research links the shock to lasting low institutional trust and later populism).
- **Why it matters / caveat:** Shows a wealthy democracy surviving a dramatic institutional rupture by reconstituting. Flag explicitly: Italy's pre-crisis institutional base was *not* genuinely top-15-20% globally because of corruption, so it is an imperfect fit for the "top-tier" requirement — included as the closest available historical rupture-and-reconstitution case.
- **Data availability:** Pre/early-WGI; rich historical and political-science scholarship.

## Synthesis: What the Cases Collectively Say

### Pattern 1 — The Convergence Qualifier: PARTIALLY HOLDS
The directional refinement is a genuine improvement over a raw gap measure. The two clearest "dangerous" cases (Greece, Nicaragua) both featured institutions falling or lagging while income held, and both reached crisis. The protective configuration (Ireland's and Botswana's high institutions) buffered shocks that could have become crises.

**But the pattern breaks or is heavily qualified in three ways:**
1. **Petro-authoritarian durability (strongest disconfirmation):** Equatorial Guinea and Gabon ran extreme widening gaps for ~27–30 years without the predicted crisis. Resource rents are an omitted variable that lets a state run a huge gap indefinitely. Any gap-based crisis predictor must condition on rent dependence.
2. **False convergence (Mali):** A "closing gap" offered zero protection because the closure was a donor-financed measurement artifact. The pattern's "closing = protective" claim is only valid if the institutional improvement is *real* — which measured indicators may not capture.
3. **Lag (Gabon):** Even where a large gap eventually ended in regime change, the multi-decade lag and the succession trigger defeat both the predictive value and the causal story.

**Structural nuance the original statement misses:** It is not the *direction* of P1 movement that matters most, but *what is driving it* — real institutional development vs. aid-financed façade vs. rent-financed repression. Direction is a noisy proxy for the underlying driver.

### Pattern 2 — The Accountability Gap: LARGELY DOES NOT HOLD AS STATED
This is the pattern with the most disconfirming evidence. Iran 1979 and Romania 1989 are real, vivid confirmations — high-capacity unaccountable states that shattered when they needed to self-correct. **But three of five cases (Oman, China 1978–2012, Cuba) directly weaken it:** all are high-capacity states with no accountability mechanism that nonetheless executed leadership transitions smoothly.

**The strongest disconfirming evidence:** Oman 2020 and China's Jiang→Hu→Xi transitions show that *institutionalized succession rules* substitute for accountability. The decisive variable is not "is there a mechanism for peaceful self-correction via popular accountability" but "is there *any* pre-committed, legitimate succession rule." Iran and Romania lacked both accountability *and* a credible succession rule; Oman, China (pre-2018), and Cuba lacked accountability but *had* a succession rule.

**Structural nuance:** The hypothesis should be reformulated. The distinct authoritarian failure mode is real, but it is triggered by the absence of *institutionalized succession*, not the absence of *accountability*. This also explains the China complication: by destroying the succession norms (2018), Xi has re-opened the vulnerability — meaning the pattern may have predictive value going *forward* even though it failed to describe 1978–2012. Two distinct sub-mechanisms also appear: succession-triggered collapse (Romania, potentially post-Xi China) vs. legitimacy/grievance-triggered collapse (Iran, Nicaragua) — the cases that test "succession" cleanly (Oman, Cuba) do not yet test "legitimacy shock" cleanly.

### Democratic-Drift Gap: REAL BUT EVIDENTIALLY THIN — ITSELF A FINDING
The premise behind the coverage gap is corroborated by how hard it is to fill: sustained, *measured* institutional decline from a genuinely top-tier base is historically rare. Israel is the one clean contemporary case, and it is still unfolding. The two historical near-analogs both *reconstituted* rather than collapsed: Iceland absorbed a catastrophic economic shock with only a modest, partly-reversed institutional dip, and Italy survived a total party-system rupture by rebuilding within constitutional bounds.

**Strongest disconfirming evidence for a pessimistic reading:** Iceland and (to a lesser extent) Italy suggest top-tier democracies possess self-correction reservoirs — independent prosecutions, peaceful transitions, constitutional continuity — that the MI's lower-base crisis cases lacked. This implies the US/Israel concern is real but that the base rate of *crisis* (as opposed to *erosion-then-stabilization*) from the top tier may be low.

**Structural nuance:** "Institutional erosion from a high base" and "institutional crisis" are distinct outcomes that the drift gap conflates. The high base appears to change the *distribution of outcomes* — making erosion more likely to resolve in reconstitution than collapse — rather than simply lowering crisis probability. (A related signal worth noting: V-Dem's 2024–2026 reports show several other long-standing democracies losing liberal-democracy status from a high base — Mauritius in 2014, Botswana in 2021, plus Portugal, Cyprus and Slovenia in the 2024 cycle — but most show partial recovery, reinforcing the "erosion-then-reconstitution" reading over "high-base crisis.")

### Uneven Evidence Across Patterns (a finding in itself)
- **Pattern 1** has the richest and most balanced evidence — strong confirmations *and* strong disconfirmations.
- **Pattern 2** has adequate evidence but it points *against* the hypothesis as stated; the reformulation (succession rules, not accountability) is the real takeaway.
- **The drift gap** is genuinely evidence-poor, which validates the original observation that the dataset lacks this archetype. Israel is carrying most of the analytical weight, and it is an in-progress case.

## Recommendations

1. **Reformulate Pattern 1 to condition on the driver of institutional change, not just its direction.** Add a rent-dependence control: in high-rent states (oil/diamonds/strategic aid), the gap-crisis link is suppressed for decades. Stage the test: (a) classify each case's P1 movement as *real reform*, *aid-financed façade*, or *rent-financed stasis*; (b) re-run the crisis prediction within each class. **Threshold to change the recommendation:** if rent-dependent states still show the expected gap-crisis link once you control for a commodity-price collapse trigger, then rents are a *timing* variable, not a *suppressor*, and the original pattern stands.

2. **Reformulate Pattern 2 around institutionalized succession rather than accountability.** Re-code each high-capacity authoritarian case for the presence/absence of a *pre-committed, elite-legitimate succession rule* (Oman's Basic Law, China's term norms, Cuba's party process) separately from accountability. **Benchmark:** if cases with a succession rule survive transitions and cases without one (Iran, Romania, post-2018 China) do not, adopt the succession-rule formulation and retire the accountability framing. Treat post-2018 China as the key prospective test.

3. **Add Israel as the live top-tier-drift anchor and Iceland/Italy as the reconstitution comparators — but tag all three as "outcome-open or reconstituted," not "crisis."** Do not code the drift gap as predicting crisis until a top-tier democracy actually reaches crisis. **Threshold:** treat a top-tier case as confirming a *crisis* pattern only if it crosses into electoral-authoritarian territory (V-Dem reclassification *plus* a non-reversed multi-year decline ≥ ~0.1 LDI). Israel is the case to watch against this threshold (its 2022→2023 LDI drop of ~0.02 and reclassification to electoral democracy are the early markers; it has not yet crossed into autocracy).

4. **Prioritize collecting the missing "legitimacy-shock" tests for Pattern 2.** The disconfirming cases (Oman, Cuba) test succession but not popular-grievance shocks. Seek high-capacity unaccountable states that faced a mass-legitimacy crisis *without* a succession trigger to isolate the two sub-mechanisms.

5. **Explicitly down-weight Pattern 2's confidence relative to Pattern 1.** Given the disconfirming balance, present Pattern 2 as a hypothesis under revision, not a validated structural pattern.

## Caveats

- **Pre-WGI cases (Iran, Romania, Italy) depend on proxy data** (Polity, V-Dem back-series, ICRG, historical scholarship). V-Dem recalculates its full back-series annually, so exact index values drift ±0.01–0.02 between dataset versions; treat historical scores as approximate.
- **Authoritarian opacity** limits structural assessment for Equatorial Guinea, Oman, and Cuba — internal elite dynamics are partly inferred.
- **Several cases sit at the boundary of two patterns** (Gabon and Nicaragua bridge Patterns 1 and 2; China bridges confirmation and disconfirmation). This is analytically useful but means clean single-pattern coding is sometimes forced.
- **Italy is an imperfect "top-tier" fit** due to endemic pre-crisis corruption; included as the closest historical rupture-and-reconstitution case, with the limitation flagged.
- **Selection awareness:** the cases were chosen to test, not confirm. The disconfirming cases (Equatorial Guinea, Mali, Oman, China, Cuba, Iceland) were deliberately sought, consistent with the truth-over-confirmation mandate.
- **Two patterns rest on in-progress outcomes** (Israel's erosion, China's post-2018 succession vulnerability); their coding may need revision as events unfold.
- **Exclusion-list compliance:** all 15 primary cases avoid the 70-case corpus; excluded countries (e.g., Hungary, Poland, Russia, Venezuela, Singapore) are referenced only for comparison.
