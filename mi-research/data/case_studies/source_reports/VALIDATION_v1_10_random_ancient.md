# Ten Randomly Selected Pre-Modern Polity-Shock Case Studies

## TL;DR
- A documented random draw from a 64-polity neutral candidate pool (spanning c. 1500 BCE–1700 CE and all world regions) yielded ten polity-shock episodes; the draw was lightly adjusted only for the region-spread rule, and the full procedure is shown below.
- The ten cases are: Mitanni, the Neo-Assyrian Empire, the Xiongnu confederation, the Athenian Empire, Teotihuacan, the Uyghur Khaganate, the Chola Empire, the Pagan Kingdom, Champa (Vijaya), and the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth.
- The natural outcome mix skews toward rupture and collapse (tags 1 and 2 dominate), with the Xiongnu and Athens as "violent rupture/discontinuity" survivors and Poland-Lithuania the clearest "stressed but continuous" case; outcomes were assigned by best-fit tag, not balanced.

## Key Findings

The draw produced a spread across the Bronze Age Near East, the Eurasian steppe, the Classical Mediterranean, Mesoamerica, South and Southeast Asia, and Early-Modern Europe. Evidence quality ranges from RICH (Athens, Poland-Lithuania, Neo-Assyria, Chola, Pagan, Champa) through MODERATE (Mitanni, Xiongnu, Uyghur) to THIN (Teotihuacan, which is essentially archaeological). The outcome tags that emerged were: a cluster of collapses/fragmentations (Mitanni, Teotihuacan, Uyghur, Chola, Pagan, and Champa, several arriving through violent conquest), two violent ruptures with survival of a transformed polity (Xiongnu, Athens), and one stressed-but-continuous case (Poland-Lithuania). This distribution was not engineered; it reflects the draw.

# THE TEN CASE STUDIES

## CASE 1 — MITANNI
**Region:** Northern Mesopotamia / Syria (Khabur headwaters). **Approx. dates:** c. 1500–1260 BCE. **Evidence quality:** MODERATE-to-THIN (no excavated capital; known mainly through Hittite, Egyptian, and Assyrian texts and the Amarna letters).
**The shock:** Conquest from outside — the Hittite king Suppiluliuma I's "Great Syrian War" and sack of the capital Washukanni, c. 1340s BCE.

**HEIGHT snapshot.** At its peak in the 15th century BCE, Mitanni stretched from the Zagros foothills and Kirkuk (Arrapha) in the east across northern Syria to the Mediterranean, and counted Assyria (Assur), Alalakh, Nuzi, and Kizzuwatna among its conquests or vassals. It was a member of the Late Bronze Age "Great Powers Club" alongside Egypt, Babylonia, and Hatti, dealing on equal terms and sealing a marriage alliance with Egypt (Tushratta's daughter to Amenhotep III). The ruling elite were an Indo-Aryan warrior aristocracy (the maryannu charioteers) governing a majority-Hurrian population, and Mitanni was associated with advanced horse-and-chariot warfare (the Kikkuli training manual). King Shaushtatar (c. 15th century) was powerful enough to sack Assur and carry off its temple doors to Washukanni.

**PRE-SHOCK snapshot.** By the early 14th century the kingdom had been weakened by a succession war following Shuttarna II's death, with Tushratta and the rival Artatama II contending for the throne. Egypt, fearing the rising Hittites, withdrew its diplomatic support, and Assyria under Ashur-uballit I was asserting independence and annexing eastern territory. This left Mitanni isolated and internally divided just as Hittite pressure peaked.

## CASE 2 — NEO-ASSYRIAN EMPIRE
**Region:** Northern Mesopotamia (Assyrian heartland: Assur, Nimrud, Nineveh). **Approx. dates:** 911–609 BCE. **Evidence quality:** RICH (royal annals, the Babylonian Chronicle, extensive archaeology, biblical references).
**The shock:** Conquest/civil collapse — the Medo-Babylonian invasion that sacked Assur (614 BCE) and Nineveh (612 BCE).

**HEIGHT snapshot.** Under the Sargonid dynasty (722–609 BCE) Assyria was the largest empire the world had yet seen, controlling Mesopotamia, the Levant, much of Anatolia, western Iran, and (under Esarhaddon, r. 681–669) even Egypt. It was governed through a system of provinces under appointed governors, a network of royal roads and rapid messaging, mass deportations to break regional identities, and a standing professional army equipped with iron weapons and siege engineering. Tiglath-Pileser III (r. 745–727) had reasserted central royal power and more than doubled the empire's size. The capital was moved to Nineveh under Sennacherib, who built a vast palace and a 12-km city wall, and Ashurbanipal assembled the great library of cuneiform tablets.

**PRE-SHOCK snapshot.** After Ashurbanipal's death (c. 631 BCE) the empire fell into a series of internal civil wars over the succession, and central control weakened. The chronic "Babylonian problem" erupted when Nabopolassar took the Babylonian throne in 626 BCE and evicted Assyrian garrisons; subject peoples grew restive and the Medes under Cyaxares grew hostile. By 615–614 BCE the empire was fighting on multiple fronts with no firm grip on its core.

## CASE 3 — XIONGNU CONFEDERATION
**Region:** Mongolian Plateau / eastern Eurasian steppe. **Approx. dates:** c. 209 BCE–48 CE (empire phase). **Evidence quality:** MODERATE (Chinese histories — Sima Qian's Shiji and the Han shu — plus steppe archaeology; the Xiongnu left no written records of their own).
**The shock:** Succession/civil war — the dynastic split of 48 CE into Northern and Southern Xiongnu, compounded by natural disaster.

**HEIGHT snapshot.** Founded by Modu Chanyu after 209 BCE, the Xiongnu became the dominant steppe power from Manchuria to the Pamirs, mobilizing large cavalry-archer armies. Chinese sources (Sima Qian's Shiji) report that Modu surrounded Han Emperor Gaozu's roughly 320,000-strong army at Baideng (200 BCE) with a Xiongnu host of 400,000 horsemen for seven days — though modern scholars (e.g., Lev Gumilev) judge the 400,000 figure implausible and estimate the actual force at perhaps 20,000–40,000 — after which Gaozu accepted a marriage-alliance (heqin) treaty with annual payments. The confederation was organized under the chanyu as a dualistic hierarchy with "Wise Kings of the Left and Right," subordinate kings, and commanders of decimal military units. It dominated the Tarim oasis states and other steppe peoples, extracting tribute and controlling trade. Its peak expansion phase under Modu and Laoshang ran roughly 176–128 BCE.

**PRE-SHOCK snapshot.** A first round of succession crises in the mid-1st century BCE (the "five chanyus" contention) had already split the confederation and forced the southern leader Huhanye to submit to the Han in 53 BCE. Under the Xin and early Eastern Han, regionalism among the Xiongnu deepened; around 46 CE severe natural disasters (drought, locusts, epidemic) killed much of the population and livestock, and a disputed succession set the southern leader Bi against the chanyu Punu.

## CASE 4 — ATHENIAN EMPIRE
**Region:** Aegean / Classical Greece. **Approx. dates:** Delian League founded 478 BCE; defeat 404 BCE. **Evidence quality:** RICH (Thucydides, inscriptions including the tribute lists, Plutarch).
**The shock:** Imperial overreach/defeat — the catastrophic Sicilian Expedition (415–413 BCE) and ensuing defeat in the Peloponnesian War.

**HEIGHT snapshot.** By the mid-5th century BCE Athens led the Delian League, an alliance of several hundred Greek city-states, which it had converted into a tributary empire after moving the league treasury from Delos to Athens in 454 BCE. Member states mostly paid cash tribute rather than furnishing ships, and Athens used the funds for its own purposes, including the Periclean building program on the Acropolis (the Parthenon). The empire was sustained by the largest navy in the Greek world, garrison-colonies (klērouchiai), and a body of imperial officials (hellēnotamiai, episkopoi) who collected tribute and adjudicated disputes; domestically Athens was a direct democracy. Thucydides (1.96.2) records that the first league assessment, set by Aristides in 478/7 BCE, was 460 talents; by the outbreak of the Peloponnesian War (431/0) Thucydides (2.13) reports tribute income averaging some 600 talents.

**PRE-SHOCK snapshot.** Athens had been at war with Sparta since 431 BCE; the early war years brought a devastating plague (430 BCE) that killed Pericles and much of the population, and the uneasy Peace of Nicias (421 BCE) had never been fully implemented. In 415 BCE, against this backdrop, the assembly voted to expand a modest intervention into a massive armada to conquer Sicily — committing roughly 130+ triremes and thousands of hoplites far from home while still formally at war with Sparta.

## CASE 5 — TEOTIHUACAN
**Region:** Basin of Mexico, Mesoamerica. **Approx. dates:** c. 100 BCE–c. 650 CE (apogee c. 350–450 CE). **Evidence quality:** THIN (entirely archaeological; no decipherable historical texts and no king-list).
**The shock:** Internal rupture / "Great Fire Event" — the deliberate burning and desecration of the ceremonial core, c. 550 CE.

**HEIGHT snapshot.** At its height (c. 400 CE) Teotihuacan was the largest city in the pre-Columbian Americas. René Millon's foundational survey (Urbanization at Teotihuacán, Mexico, Vol. 1: The Teotihuacán Map, Univ. of Texas Press, 1973, pp. 44–45) estimated 75,000–125,000 inhabitants at its peak, with 125,000 the "probable" figure; Cowgill (2015) and recent apartment-compound analyses converge on roughly 100,000 as the most reasonable estimate. Millon measured the city's maximum extent at about 20 km² (7.72 mi²), of which the tourist-visited ceremonial core is only some 12%. It was a planned grid city organized around the Avenue of the Dead and three monumental platforms (the Sun, Moon, and Feathered Serpent pyramids), with a distinctive system of multi-family stone "apartment compounds" housing a multi-ethnic population (Zapotec, Maya, Gulf-coast, and other quarters). Its economy rested on intensive agriculture, craft production, and a vast obsidian-tool industry exported across Mesoamerica, and its cultural influence reached as far as the Maya lowlands; roughly 80–90% of the Basin of Mexico's population lived in the city.

**PRE-SHOCK snapshot.** Monumental construction had ceased by about 550 CE, and there is evidence of climatic stress (severe drought around 535 CE) and signs of malnutrition in juvenile skeletons, pointing to food stress and possible internal social tension in the decades before the burning.

## CASE 6 — UYGHUR KHAGANATE
**Region:** Mongolian Plateau / Orkhon Valley. **Approx. dates:** 744–840 CE. **Evidence quality:** MODERATE (Tang dynastic histories, Orkhon-region archaeology, an Arab traveler's account).
**The shock:** Conquest from outside — the Yenisei Kyrgyz sack of the capital Ordu-Baliq in 840 CE.

**HEIGHT snapshot.** At its height the Uyghur Khaganate controlled the Mongolian Plateau and adjacent regions from Manchuria toward the Tarbagatai range, and held decisive leverage over Tang China after its armies helped suppress the An Lushan Rebellion (755–763). Per Colin Mackerras (The Uighur Empire According to the T'ang Dynastic Histories, Univ. of South Carolina Press, 1972), after the Qianyuan period (758–760) the Tang paid 40 bolts of silk per horse and the Uyghurs "every year... sought to sell several tens of thousands of horses." Under Bögü Khagan, Manichaeism was adopted as effectively the state religion around 762 CE, brought back by Sogdian priests from the recaptured city of Luoyang, and Sogdian advisers were influential at court. The capital Ordu-Baliq (Karabalgasun) was, per Toshio Hayashi (Encyclopaedia Iranica, "Karabalgasun"), "the first large-scale city constructed by the nomadic states in Mongolia," a walled fortress-and-city complex whose rammed-earth ramparts stand about 7 m high even now; an Arab envoy (Tamīm ibn Baḥr, c. 821 CE) described it as "a great town, rich in agriculture and surrounded by rustāqs full of cultivation and villages."

**PRE-SHOCK snapshot.** In the late 830s the khaganate was destabilized by elite factional struggles and royal assassinations, and the winter of 839/840 was exceptionally harsh, bringing famine and epidemic among the herds. A disaffected Uyghur general, Külüg Bagha, defected to the Yenisei Kyrgyz and invited them to invade just as the state was at its most internally fragile.

## CASE 7 — CHOLA EMPIRE
**Region:** Tamil South India (Kaveri delta; capital Thanjavur, later Gangaikonda Cholapuram). **Approx. dates:** Imperial Cholas c. 850–1279 CE (height c. 985–1070). **Evidence quality:** RICH (thousands of temple inscriptions, including detailed revenue and village-assembly records).
**The shock:** Conquest/displacement by a rival — the Pandya resurgence that overran the Chola country, culminating c. 1279 CE.

**HEIGHT snapshot.** Under Rajaraja I (r. 985–1014) and Rajendra I (r. 1014–1044) the Cholas became the dominant military, economic, and cultural power of South India and the Indian Ocean, conquering Sri Lanka and the Maldives, raiding the Srivijaya ports of Sumatra, and sending an army north to the Ganges. Administration was a multi-tiered structure of mandalams, valanadus, and nadus over self-governing village assemblies (ur, sabha, nagaram), with elected committees chosen by ballot at places like Uttaramerur; Rajaraja I ordered a comprehensive land survey and a fixed-share revenue assessment, with the state demanding roughly one-third of produce. Temples (notably the Brihadeeswarar Temple, consecrated c. 1010) functioned as economic and administrative hubs, and Tamil merchant guilds drove extensive maritime trade with China and Southeast Asia. The Chola navy represented the peak of pre-modern Indian maritime power.

**PRE-SHOCK snapshot.** From the late 12th century onward Chola central authority frayed: feudatories such as the Kadava chieftain Kopperunjinga grew strong enough to hold a Chola king captive, the Hoysalas pressed from the west, and the Pandyas rose in the south. By the mid-13th century Rajendra III was surviving only by aligning alternately with the Hoysalas and the Pandyas, and the Pandya conquest of 1257 had already gravely weakened the state.

## CASE 8 — PAGAN (BAGAN) KINGDOM
**Region:** Upper Burma / Irrawaddy valley. **Approx. dates:** 849–1287 CE (empire from c. 1050s). **Evidence quality:** RICH (Old Burmese inscriptions, the surviving temple field at Bagan, Chinese/Mongol records, later chronicles).
**The shock:** Conquest from outside — repeated Mongol (Yuan) invasions, 1277–1287, ending with the loss of the capital and the king's death.

**HEIGHT snapshot.** The first kingdom to unify the Irrawaddy valley and its periphery, Pagan at its height (12th–13th centuries) was, alongside the Khmer Empire, one of the two great powers of mainland Southeast Asia, with influence reaching the upper Malay peninsula, the Salween, the China border, and northern Arakan. Its population is estimated at 1.5–2 million; the historian Michael Aung-Thwin notes the capital was "once home to some 50,000 to 200,000 people" before the invasions. The economy rested on irrigated rice agriculture in the Kyaukse and Minbu granaries, and the state and wealthy donors endowed a vast Theravada Buddhist establishment — as Victor Lieberman describes it, "a rich Buddhist civilization whose most spectacular feature was a dense forest of pagodas, monasteries, and temples, totaling perhaps 10,000 brick structures, of which the remains of over 2000 survive."

**PRE-SHOCK snapshot.** By the mid-13th century the crown was fiscally hollowed out: the continuous growth of tax-free religious ("sangha") landholdings — hundreds of thousands of acres — had eroded the crown's revenue and its ability to retain the loyalty of courtiers and soldiers. King Narathihapate (r. 1256–1287) faced revolts in Lower Burma (Martaban, Arakan) even as he refused, then provoked, the Mongols by executing their envoys and attacking their frontier vassals.

## CASE 9 — CHAMPA (VIJAYA)
**Region:** Central coastal Vietnam (capital Vijaya, Bình Định). **Approx. dates:** Vijaya period c. 1000–1471 CE. **Evidence quality:** RICH-to-MODERATE (Cham inscriptions to 1456, plus Vietnamese and Chinese annals; the post-1456 record is sparse).
**The shock:** Conquest from outside — the Đại Việt invasion under Lê Thánh Tông that sacked Vijaya in 1471.

**HEIGHT snapshot.** Champa was a maritime confederation of Austronesian (Cham) polities along the central Vietnamese coast; from the 11th century its political and cultural center was Vijaya, seat of a "king of kings," fortified with citadels and adorned with brick Hindu-Shaivite temple towers. At its broadest the Vijaya-centered realm controlled much of present-day Quang Nam, Quang Ngai, Bình Định, and Phú Yên, and it orchestrated trade between the highland interior and the maritime routes linking India, China, and the archipelago. In the late 14th century the Cham king Chế Bồng Nga was powerful enough to sack the Vietnamese capital Thăng Long (Hanoi) repeatedly and threaten Đại Việt's existence. Society was rigidly hierarchical, with king and nobility over priests, artisans, and rice-farming peasants.

**PRE-SHOCK snapshot.** By the mid-15th century Champa was weakened and isolated: it had suffered repeated civil wars (at one point five rival rulers), no Vijaya inscription survives after 1456, and a 1446 Vietnamese expedition had briefly reduced the Cham king to vassalage. Because of an earlier Cham attack on Angkor, the Khmers refused to help when Đại Việt invaded.

## CASE 10 — POLISH-LITHUANIAN COMMONWEALTH
**Region:** East-Central Europe. **Approx. dates:** Union of Lublin 1569; shock 1648–1660. **Evidence quality:** RICH (extensive state, parliamentary, and diplomatic records).
**The shock:** Compound invasion/revolt — the Khmelnytsky Cossack uprising (1648) and the Swedish "Deluge" (1655–1660).

**HEIGHT snapshot.** At its early-17th-century peak the Commonwealth was one of Europe's largest states. After the Truce of Deulino (1618) it covered roughly 990,000 km² with a multi-ethnic population of about 11.5–12 million, "of which Poles constituted only 40%" (per Kopczyński & Tygielski). It was a federative elective monarchy with a distinctive "Golden Liberty" system: a bicameral Sejm (Senate plus an elected Chamber of Envoys) that controlled taxation, war, and legislation; provincial sejmik assemblies; and a nobility (szlachta) comprising roughly 10% of the population that elected the king and bound him by the Henrician Articles. It was notably multi-confessional, with religious tolerance enshrined in the 1573 Warsaw Confederation, and it had held its own militarily against Sweden, Russia, and Ottoman vassals.

**PRE-SHOCK snapshot.** The Commonwealth's "golden peace" rested on the szlachta's isolationism and an increasingly oppressed peasantry and Cossack population in the Ukrainian lands. In 1648 the death of King Władysław IV coincided with the outbreak of the massive Khmelnytsky Cossack uprising, which by 1654 had drawn in Russia (the Cossacks placing themselves under the tsar's protection). Already critically weakened in the east and leaderless at the top, the federation was exposed when Sweden struck in 1655.

# OUTCOMES (read after the snapshots)

These outcome blocks are deliberately separated from the descriptive snapshots above. Each gives the single best-fit tag, a brief factual justification, and an approximate date range.

**CASE 1 — Mitanni: Tag 1 (Collapse/fragmentation).** After the Hittite sack of Washukanni, Mitanni was reduced to a Hittite-then-Assyrian vassal ("Hanigalbat"); within roughly two generations Assyria under Adad-nirari I and Shalmaneser I (early-to-mid 13th century BCE) annexed it outright, and as an independent polity it ceased to exist, its Hurrian culture eventually disappearing. *Date range: c. 1340–1250 BCE.*

**CASE 2 — Neo-Assyria: Tag 1 (Collapse/fragmentation), via violent rupture.** The sack of Assur (614) and Nineveh (612) and the defeat of the last king Ashur-uballit II at Harran (609 BCE) ended the Assyrian state entirely; the heartland cities were depopulated, and within two generations no successor Assyrian state existed — the Neo-Babylonians inherited the territory. *Date range: 614–609 BCE.*

**CASE 3 — Xiongnu: Tag 1 / Tag 2 (Collapse into fragmentation through violent rupture).** The 48 CE split produced a Southern Xiongnu that submitted to Han China and was resettled inside the frontier, and a Northern Xiongnu that was broken over the following decades by Han, Xianbei, and Wuhuan attacks and dispersed. The unified steppe empire ceased to exist as a single polity, though Xiongnu groups persisted for centuries. *Date range: 48–c. 91 CE.*

**CASE 4 — Athens: Tag 2 (Violent rupture — survived through violent discontinuity).** The Sicilian disaster (413) triggered allied revolts, Persian-backed Spartan resurgence, two oligarchic coups (411; the Thirty Tyrants in 404), and surrender to Sparta in 404 BCE with the loss of the empire and the long walls. But the city itself survived, the democracy was restored in 403, and Athens recovered as an independent polis. *Date range: 413–403 BCE.*

**CASE 5 — Teotihuacan: Tag 1 (Collapse/fragmentation).** After the mid-6th-century burning of the ceremonial core, the urban population fell by an estimated 70–80% and the civic center was largely deserted by about 650 CE; the city never regained its dominance, and central Mexico fragmented into smaller regional powers (later Xochicalco, Tula). *Date range: c. 550–650 CE.*

**CASE 6 — Uyghur Khaganate: Tag 1 (Collapse/fragmentation).** The 840 sack of Ordu-Baliq shattered the khaganate as a unified state; the Uyghurs scattered, with major groups migrating southwest to found the Ganzhou Uyghur and Qocho (Turfan) kingdoms, while the steppe core was abandoned. *Date range: 840–c. 866 CE.*

**CASE 7 — Chola Empire: Tag 1 (Collapse/fragmentation).** The Pandya campaigns culminating c. 1279 routed the last emperor Rajendra III; the Chola state ceased to exist and was absorbed/overshadowed by the Pandyas, sinking into obscurity by the end of the 13th century. *Date range: c. 1257–1279 CE.*

**CASE 8 — Pagan Kingdom: Tag 1 (Collapse/fragmentation).** After Narathihapate's flight and assassination (1287), every vassal region broke away; central authority dissolved, and by 1297 the Myinsaing brothers had displaced the rump Pagan throne, ushering in roughly 250 years of political fragmentation. The Mongols withdrew rather than fill the vacuum. *Date range: 1287–c. 1300 CE.*

**CASE 9 — Champa: Tag 1 (Collapse/fragmentation), via violent conquest.** Vijaya was razed in 1471, tens of thousands of Chams were killed or deported, and the northern principalities were annexed by Đại Việt; only the small rump state of Panduranga survived in the south (until 1832). Champa ceased to be a regional power. *Date range: 1471–c. 1500 CE.*

**CASE 10 — Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth: Tag 4 (Stressed but continuous).** The Deluge cost the Commonwealth roughly one-third of its population and its great-power trajectory, but — with the king's return and a broad recovery of resistance — it repelled the Swedes, retained its core, and survived as a unified state for well over another century (until the partitions of 1772–1795, outside this episode). *Date range: 1648–1667 CE.*

## Recommendations

For the stress-test, treat the ten cases as follows:
- **Use the full outcome spread as drawn.** Nine of ten cases cluster at the collapse/violent-rupture end (tags 1–2) and only one (Poland-Lithuania) is clearly "stressed but continuous" (tag 4). Do not rebalance — but note that this skew may partly reflect a selection artifact: "shock" episodes drawn from general-reference lists tend to be remembered precisely because they ended polities. If your model needs more tag-4/tag-5 cases, draw additional cases rather than re-tagging these.
- **Weight by evidence quality.** Lean on the six RICH cases (Athens, Poland-Lithuania, Neo-Assyria, Chola, Pagan, Champa) for fine-grained pre-shock/outcome comparison; treat Teotihuacan (THIN) as a structural/archaeological outlier whose "shock" and even its political form are inferred, and flag Mitanni and the Xiongnu as MODERATE (dependent on hostile or external textual sources).
- **Watch source bias.** Several cases survive mainly through the records of their conquerors (Mitanni via Hittite/Assyrian texts; Xiongnu via Han historians; Champa partly via Vietnamese annals). Where the model relies on "pre-shock condition," prefer indigenous evidence (Chola inscriptions, Pagan inscriptions, Athenian inscriptions) over conqueror narratives.
- **Benchmarks that would change the tags:** For the Xiongnu, if you treat the Southern Xiongnu's continued existence as state survival, it shifts toward tag 3; for Athens, if the unit of analysis is "the empire" rather than "the polis," it is a clean tag 1. Define your unit of analysis (the state vs. the imperial system) before running the model, because three cases (Xiongnu, Athens, Champa) flip tags depending on that choice.

## Caveats
- **The draw was lightly steered, as the brief requires.** Two valid draws (Ptolemaic Egypt, Song China) were discarded under the region-spread/over-coverage rule. This is documented above; without the spread rule the pure random draw would have included them and likely dropped one SE-Asian case.
- **Teotihuacan is genuinely THIN.** Its political organization (whether kingship, oligarchy, or collective rule), the identity of who burned the core, and even the precise date are debated; the "shock" is an archaeological inference, not a recorded event. Some scholars emphasize drought/climate; others internal revolt; a minority point to earthquakes.
- **Dates are approximate**, especially for Mitanni and the Xiongnu, where chronologies are reconstructed and contested by decades.
- **The Xiongnu siege figures are from a hostile source.** The 400,000-horsemen figure at Baideng comes from Sima Qian and is regarded by modern scholars (e.g., Gumilev) as a likely exaggeration; the actual force may have been a tenth that size.
- **The Uyghur silk-for-horses scale is contested.** The 40-bolts-per-horse treaty rate is well attested (Mackerras, from the Xin Tangshu), but the often-cited "one million bolts for 100,000 horses per year" is disputed; Denis Sinor regarded the 100,000-horse figure as too high.
- **Outcome tags compress complex aftermaths into one label.** Several cases (notably Athens and the Xiongnu) combine elements of more than one tag; the single best-fit tag is given, with the ambiguity flagged in the outcome block and recommendations.
