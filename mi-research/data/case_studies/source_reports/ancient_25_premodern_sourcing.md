# 25 Pre-Modern Case Studies for the Modernization Index (MI): A Structural Sourcing Report

## TL;DR
- I have assembled 25 new ancient/pre-modern case studies (c. 3000 BCE–1800 CE) spanning sub-Saharan Africa, South/Southeast/East/Central Asia, Mesoamerica, the Andes, the Mediterranean and the Near East, each with enough documented evidence to support qualitative scoring of the five MI pillars; 16 are RICH, 7 MODERATE, 2 lean THIN.
- The cases give strongest ancient support to the suppression-typology, porosity-vs-suppression, P4–P1 gap, and deceleration-container findings, and weakest support to the strict P1-ordinality and "golden ages are level-not-slope" findings, which several cases actively challenge (high-institution Sasanian Persia collapsing in under two decades; lower-maximal-P1 Venice and Joseon enduring centuries).
- The largest pattern the modern framework appears to under-weight is the **exogenous-shock and ecological-container axis**: in pre-modern cases climate/hydraulic shocks and trade-route reroutings repeatedly drove "P5" outcomes substantially independently of institutional quality, suggesting the MI needs a pre-modern environmental/geographic moderator.

## Key Findings

The 25 cases below are organized to stress the MI in all directions — confirmations, challenges, and extensions. Where evidence is contested or speculative, I flag it rather than presenting it as settled. The existing 5 cases (Western/Eastern Rome, Han, Abbasid, Mongol, Inca) are folded into the coverage map.

Three framing judgments up front:

1. **The MI's strongest pre-modern travelers are its "configuration" and "suppression" findings.** Cases where force substituted for institutional accommodation (Aztec hegemonic tribute empire, Mongol, Inca, Vijayanagara's military-fiscal apex) fragmented along exactly the fault lines they had suppressed. Cases that diffused pressure through institutional channels (Song, Byzantine Macedonian revival, Venice, Joseon, Tokugawa) showed markedly greater durability.

2. **The P1-ordinality finding ("higher institutional quality predicts better post-stress trajectory") is the most vulnerable in pre-modern data.** Several high-P1 polities collapsed faster than low-P1 ones because exogenous shocks (plague, climate, conquest-from-outside) overwhelmed institutional buffers. The finding survives only if "trajectory" is measured over long horizons and conditioned on shock type.

3. **"Golden ages are level-and-era, not slope" finds genuine but partial support.** The Song commercial revolution, the Byzantine Macedonian revival, and the Gupta classical age all show low or recovering bases catching exogenous waves — but the waves were demographic, agronomic, and printing/technology diffusion, not the global-integration waves the modern framework assumes.

## Details: The 25 Cases

### A. Egyptian and Near Eastern deep-time cases

**1. Old Kingdom Egypt → First Intermediate Period (c. 2686–2055 BCE; Nile Valley)**
- *Stress type:* Imperial collapse into self-organizing fragmentation; institutional reconstruction in the Middle Kingdom sequel.
- *MI findings probed:* Deceleration-container (confirms); suppression-vs-porosity (extends); fragment count (confirms self-organizing 2–4 range — here a north–south bipartition into Herakleopolitan and Theban blocs).
- *Structural sketch:* A highly centralized, pharaoh-apex redistributive state (P1 moderate-high for its era, P4 agrarian-redistributive) hollowed out as nomarch governorships became hereditary and the priesthood at Giza/Heliopolis accumulated wealth — institutional erosion preceding the shock. A run of low Nile floods (the 4.2-kiloyear event is debated; Manning argues "State collapse was complicated, but unrelated to Nile flooding history") delivered the deceleration; the eroding container ruptured into competing nomes. Mentuhotep II's Theban reunification (c. 2055 BCE) is the institutional-reconstruction positive sequel.
- *Sources:* Marc Van de Mieroop, *A History of Ancient Egypt*; Britannica/Old Kingdom; Würzburg "Beyond Memphis" provincial-administration conference papers; G. Mumford lectures.
- *Data quality:* RICH.

**2. Achaemenid Persia (c. 550–330 BCE; Iran, Mesopotamia, Anatolia, Egypt, Central Asia)**
- *Stress type:* Successful consolidation and institutional innovation, then conquest-from-outside (Alexander).
- *MI findings probed:* Innovation-without-institutions inverted — here institutional innovation IS the story (extends P1/P2 linkage); porosity (confirms — religious tolerance and local-law accommodation diffused pressure).
- *Structural sketch:* Darius I's satrapy system (c. 20–23 provinces), the "King's Eyes and Ears" inspectorate, Imperial Aramaic as administrative lingua franca, standardized coinage, a regulated tax system tailored to provincial productivity (Babylon assessed 1,000 silver talents; Egypt 700 talents plus 120,000 measures of grain), and the Royal Road. Very high P1 and P2 for the era; P4 a tribute-and-trade agrarian economy; P5 punctuated by satrapal revolts (the Great Satraps' Revolt 372–362 BCE) but structurally durable for two centuries. Fell to external conquest, not internal rupture — a case where high P1 did NOT prevent collapse because the stressor was exogenous military defeat.
- *Sources:* Pierre Briant, *From Cyrus to Alexander*; Encyclopaedia Iranica ("Achaemenid Satrapies"); Josef Wiesehöfer, *Ancient Persia*.
- *Data quality:* RICH.

**3. Sasanian Persia (224–651 CE; Iran, Mesopotamia)**
- *Stress type:* Violent dissolution of a high-institution empire; the framework's sharpest P1-ordinality challenge.
- *MI findings probed:* P1-ordinality (CHALLENGES — a sophisticated bureaucracy collapsed in under two decades); suppression typology / "decentralize along fault lines" (confirms via the Parthian-noble defection); deceleration-container (confirms — war-exhaustion as decelerator).
- *Structural sketch:* A centralized "King of Kings" bureaucratic monarchy with provincial governors (šahrab/ōstāndār), a vizier (wuzurg framadar), and Khosrow I Anushirvan's land-survey/fixed-tax reforms (r. 531–579) that created the dehqan landholding gentry and four military frontier quarters under spahbeds — the institutional high-water mark, very high P1. Yet collapse was fast: the exhausting 602–628 war with Byzantium (decisive defeat at Nineveh 627), the Plague of Sheroe, then succession chaos (roughly ten rulers in four years, 628–632), then the Arab conquest (al-Qadisiyyah 636, Ctesiphon 637, Nahavand 641/642; Yazdegerd III killed at Merv 651). Pourshariati's thesis: the state was a Sasanian–Parthian confederacy whose great northern/eastern houses defected and made separate peace — collapse as dissolution of an internally fractured confederacy.
- *Sources:* Touraj Daryaee, *Sasanian Persia: The Rise and Fall of an Empire* (2009); Parvaneh Pourshariati, *Decline and Fall of the Sasanian Empire* (2008); Richard N. Frye, *Cambridge History of Iran* vol. 3.1.
- *Data quality:* RICH.

### B. South Asian cases

**4. Mauryan Empire → post-Ashokan fragmentation (c. 322–185 BCE; Indian subcontinent)**
- *Stress type:* Succession-driven imperial dissolution after a consolidation apex.
- *MI findings probed:* P4–P1 gap and convergence qualifier (confirms — institutions FELL after Ashoka while territorial position stayed large, the dangerous configuration); deceleration-container (confirms via fiscal strain).
- *Structural sketch:* A highly centralized state with an Arthashastra-derived administrative blueprint (espionage, graded taxation, distinct courts), a large standing army, capital at Pataliputra — high P1 dependent on a strong ruler at the center. After Ashoka's death (232 BCE), weak successors, fiscal strain from military/administrative overhead, and provincial reassertion (Taxila, Kalinga, Gandhara) produced disintegration within ~50 years; Brihadratha was assassinated by Pushyamitra Shunga in 185 BCE. Historiographic caveat: the Arthashastra's surviving compilation post-dates Chandragupta and should not be read as a literal court manual.
- *Sources:* Romila Thapar, *Aśoka and the Decline of the Mauryas*; Patrick Olivelle (Arthashastra studies); Upinder Singh, *A History of Ancient and Early Medieval India*.
- *Data quality:* RICH.

**5. Gupta Empire — classical "golden age" and Huna-driven decline (c. 320–550 CE; northern/central India)**
- *Stress type:* Golden age / institutional ascent, then external-invasion + decentralization decline.
- *MI findings probed:* "Golden ages are level-and-era, not slope" (confirms partially — cultural/scientific apogee on a layered, less-centralized base); deceleration-container (confirms — land-grant decentralization eroded the container before the Huna shock).
- *Structural sketch:* A layered polity (directly ruled bhukti provinces, tributary vassals, allied tribes) — P1 moderate and deliberately less centralized than the Maurya; P4 rich (decimal system, Aryabhata, Mediterranean-to-Cambodia trade, strong gold coinage). The conversion of revenue rights into hereditary land grants (to samantas) eroded the fiscal core; Huna/Hephthalite invasions from c. 460s–500s and the rise of Yashodharman accelerated fragmentation. Skandagupta's victory (Bhitari/Junagadh inscriptions) bought one generation. Institutions falling + economic position high = the dangerous gap configuration.
- *Sources:* Romila Thapar, *The Penguin History of Early India*; R.C. Majumdar; Hermann Kulke & Dietmar Rothermund, *A History of India*.
- *Data quality:* RICH.

**6. Vijayanagara Empire → Battle of Talikota (1336–1565/1646; Deccan, South India)**
- *Stress type:* Military-fiscal apex then catastrophic battlefield defeat and capital sack, followed by slow decentralized contraction (nayaka successor states).
- *MI findings probed:* Suppression typology (confirms — an aggressive military-hegemonic posture toward the Deccan sultanates produced the coalition that destroyed it); fragment count (engineered/military fragmentation producing many nayaka successor states, NOT the 2–4 self-organizing range — extends).
- *Structural sketch:* Wealthy, militarized, with the nayaka provincial-military-governor system. The Portuguese chronicler Domingo Paes (c. 1520) wrote that the capital "seemed to me as large as Rome, and very beautiful to the sight... the best provided city in the world," putting its circuit at "sixty miles" with "people countless in number." P4 high (Indian Ocean horse trade, temple-urban economy); P1 moderate and personalized under the regent Aliya Rama Raya. The 1565 defeat (commander defections, artillery gap) and roughly six-month sack of Hampi crippled the core; the empire limped on under the Aravidu dynasty to 1646 while nayakas (Madurai, Tanjore, Gingee, Ikkeri) became effectively independent.
- *Sources:* Burton Stein, *Vijayanagara* (New Cambridge History of India); Richard M. Eaton, *A Social History of the Deccan*; Robert Sewell, *A Forgotten Empire* (Paes & Nunes chronicles).
- *Data quality:* RICH.

### C. East Asian cases

**7. Tang Dynasty → An Lushan Rebellion and slow decline (618–907 CE; China)**
- *Stress type:* Golden age punctured by rebellion; institutional erosion and regional-warlord (jiedushi) decentralization.
- *MI findings probed:* Suppression-vs-porosity (confirms — over-mighty frontier military governorships were the fault line); deceleration-container (confirms — equal-field-system breakdown eroded the container).
- *Structural sketch:* High-P1 civil-service state with the equal-field land system; An Lushan's 755–763 revolt (a frontier jiedushi controlling NE China) shattered central control. The court survived ~150 more years only by ceding fiscal/military authority to regional governors and leaning on the salt monopoly — a textbook case of decentralizing along the exact (military-governor) fault line that had been allowed to concentrate.
- *Sources:* Denis Twitchett (ed.), *Cambridge History of China* vol. 3; Charles Benn, *China's Golden Age*; Mark Edward Lewis, *China's Cosmopolitan Empire*.
- *Data quality:* RICH.

**8. Song Dynasty — commercial revolution and meritocratic institutions (960–1279 CE; China)**
- *Stress type:* Institutional ascent / golden age of governance after the Five Dynasties fragmentation; positive case.
- *MI findings probed:* "Golden ages are level-and-era, not slope" (confirms strongly — institution-building from the low base of the Five Dynasties chaos, catching the exogenous waves of printing, an agricultural revolution, and population growth); porosity (confirms); P2/P3 linkage (extends).
- *Structural sketch:* The civil-service examination became virtually the sole route into office; per Columbia's Asia for Educators, "the number of men entering each competition grew steadily, from fewer than 30,000 early in the dynasty, to about 400,000 by the dynasty's end," driving the pass rate "as low as one in 333 in some prefectures." Active jinshi degree-holders numbered between 5,000 and 10,000 across the 11th–13th centuries (e.g., 7,085 of 18,700 posts in 1046; 8,260 of 38,870 posts in 1213). A "fiscal state" with paper money; a commercial revolution with proto-capitalist markets; Taizu's deliberate subordination of the military to civilian control. Very high P1, P2, P3, P4 for any pre-modern polity. The Northern Song fell to the Jurchen (1127) and the Southern Song to the Mongols (1279) — external conquest, not institutional rupture. A premier positive/"durability-through-porosity" case.
- *Sources:* Dieter Kuhn, *The Age of Confucian Rule*; Robert Hartwell; Mark Elvin, *The Pattern of the Chinese Past*; Cambridge *Economic History of China*.
- *Data quality:* RICH.

**9. Tokugawa Japan — Pax Tokugawa and the Meiji transition (1600/1603–1868; Japan)**
- *Stress type:* Successful long-peace consolidation, then a comparatively peaceful institutional transition (not collapse) into the Meiji state.
- *MI findings probed:* Configuration thesis (confirms — balanced profile, very high P5); managed transition / institutional reconstruction (confirms a POSITIVE non-collapse outcome); deceleration-container (extends — fiscal stress on the bakuhan system absorbed rather than ruptured).
- *Structural sketch:* The bakuhan system — shogunal national authority over ~260–300 daimyō domains, fixed hereditary classes, sankin-kōtai hostage-rotation — produced two and a half centuries of peace, urbanization, rising literacy and commercial growth (high P3, P5; P1 a decentralized-but-effective federation). The mid-19th-c. crisis (fiscal strain + foreign pressure) ended not in dissolution but in a relatively contained 1868 coup and rapid institutional modernization — the unusually high pre-existing political/educational sophistication is what made peaceful, rapid change possible.
- *Sources:* Marius Jansen & Gilbert Rozman (eds.), *Japan in Transition: From Tokugawa to Meiji*; Conrad Totman, *Early Modern Japan*; Susan B. Hanley on material conditions.
- *Data quality:* RICH.

**10. Joseon Korea — Confucian-bureaucratic longevity (1392–1910; Korean peninsula)**
- *Stress type:* Exceptional institutional durability (518 years) through factional stress and the Imjin War invasion + reconstruction.
- *MI findings probed:* P1-ordinality (confirms over long horizon); configuration thesis (confirms — balanced, stable profile); institutional reconstruction (confirms — recovery after the 1592–98 Hideyoshi invasions); possible CHALLENGE — a moderate-not-maximal P1 polity achieving exceptional longevity.
- *Structural sketch:* A Neo-Confucian bureaucratic monarchy with the gwageo civil-service exam, the yangban aristocratic class, censorate institutions, magistrate-administered counties, and royal authority checked by yangban factions. P1 moderate-high, P5 generally high despite the bloody sahwa literati purges and chronic factionalism. The devastating Imjin War (1592–1598) was followed by land surveys, tax and military reforms, and reassertion of the bureaucracy — institutional reconstruction rather than collapse. Longevity through institutionalized factional channeling (porosity), though late-dynasty ossification is a caveat.
- *Sources:* Martina Deuchler, *The Confucian Transformation of Korea*; John Duncan, *The Origins of the Joseon Dynasty*; James B. Palais, *Confucian Statecraft and Korean Institutions*.
- *Data quality:* RICH.

### D. Southeast Asian cases

**11. Khmer Empire / Angkor → southward shift (802–1431 CE; Cambodia/mainland SE Asia)**
- *Stress type:* Peaceful decline / managed relocation (NOT sudden collapse), driven by hydraulic-infrastructure failure under climate stress.
- *MI findings probed:* Deceleration-container (confirms strongly — economic deceleration meeting an eroding ecological/infrastructural container); the environmental moderator the MI under-weights (extends).
- *Structural sketch:* One of the largest pre-industrial urban complexes, organized around a vast baray-and-canal hydraulic network underwriting rice surplus and the devaraja kingship. P4 high (hydraulic agriculture + temple economy); P1 moderate, ritual-kingship-dependent. Buckley et al. (PNAS 2010) found that Angkor "experienced decades-long drought interspersed with intense monsoons in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries that, in combination with other factors, contributed to its eventual demise," based on "a seven-and-a-half century robust hydroclimate reconstruction from tropical southern Vietnamese tree rings." Fletcher's "scale and interconnectedness" thesis holds the over-built network was hard to modify; population shifted toward the river/delta trade economy, and Angkor Wat persisted as a religious site. Adaptive relocation, not catastrophic rupture.
- *Sources:* Buckley et al., "Climate as a Contributing Factor in the Demise of Angkor," *PNAS* 107(15):6748–6752 (2010); Damian Evans; Roland Fletcher; Bernard-Philippe Groslier (hydraulic-city thesis).
- *Data quality:* RICH.

**12. Srivijaya — maritime thalassocracy and its unwinding (c. 670–1025/13th c.; Sumatra, Strait of Malacca)**
- *Stress type:* Conquest-raid shock to a thin-institution chokepoint hegemony, then mandala "running in reverse."
- *MI findings probed:* P4–P1 gap (confirms strongly — extreme commercial position on minimal territorial institutions, the structurally crisis-prone configuration); fragment count (extends — rapid defection of subordinate ports).
- *Structural sketch:* A thalassocracy controlling the China–India route chokepoints; authority rested on command of trade nodes, redistribution, and ritual prestige (Wolters' "men of prowess"), organized as a mandala of port-polities with non-exclusive loyalties — P4 very high, P1 very thin (no territorial bureaucracy). The Chola naval raid of 1025 (Rajendra Chola I) sacked Palembang and captured the king; the contingent subordinates, joined only for trade benefits, defected and the mandala unwound. A near-pure illustration that a huge P4–P1 gap makes crisis structurally probable.
- *Sources:* O.W. Wolters, *The Fall of Srivijaya in Malay History* (1970) and *History, Culture, and Region in Southeast Asian Perspectives* (1982/1999); Kenneth R. Hall, *A History of Early Southeast Asia* (2011); George Cœdès, *The Indianized States of Southeast Asia* (1968).
- *Data quality:* MODERATE.

**13. Majapahit — Javanese apogee and post-apogee fragmentation (c. 1293–1500; Java/archipelago)**
- *Stress type:* Consolidation apex under Hayam Wuruk/Gajah Mada, then succession-driven civil war and Islamization.
- *MI findings probed:* Configuration thesis (confirms — apex dependent on two individuals, fragile profile); suppression typology (extends — coercive mandala over claimed tributaries); deceleration-container (confirms via post-1389 succession rupture).
- *Structural sketch:* A dual-base mandala — rice surplus from interior Java PLUS spice transshipment — stronger institutionally than Srivijaya but still a concentric mandala (direct royal domain, tributaries, trade allies). The Nagarakretagama (Mpu Prapanca, 1365) claims 104 tributaries; modern historians DEBATE whether these were administered provinces or mere trade partners (flagged as eulogistic, not administered fact). Decline set in immediately after Gajah Mada (d. 1364) and Hayam Wuruk (d. 1389): the Paregreg civil war (1405–06) and the spread of coastal Islamic sultanates fragmented it by ~1500.
- *Sources:* O.W. Wolters; Kenneth R. Hall; the Nagarakretagama (Deśawarṇana) and Pararaton as primary texts; Theodore Pigeaud, *Java in the 14th Century*.
- *Data quality:* MODERATE.

### E. Sub-Saharan African cases

**14. Mali Empire — Mansa Musa apex and post-1360 decline (c. 1230–1600; West Africa, Niger bend)**
- *Stress type:* Golden-age commercial/cultural apex, then succession-driven slow decline and absorption by Songhai.
- *MI findings probed:* Deceleration-container (confirms — trade-route reorientation as decelerator, weak succession as eroding container); P4–P1 gap (confirms — vast commercial wealth on a personalized, succession-fragile institutional base).
- *Structural sketch:* A centralized-but-personalized imperial network: the Mansa as political-spiritual apex, provincial governors, a council of advisors, and Islamic-scholarly administrative legitimation (Timbuktu, the Sankoré madrasa). P4 very high (trans-Saharan gold-salt trade); P1 moderate and dependent on orderly succession, which failed repeatedly after Mansa Suleyman's death (1360). The empire frayed as it lost control of trade towns to Songhai; reduced to its Malinké core by the 1500s. Ibn Battuta (1352) and the Tarikh al-Sudan are key witnesses.
- *Sources:* Nehemia Levtzion, *Ancient Ghana and Mali*; Ibn Battuta (Hamdun & King trans.); Michael Gomez, *African Dominion*.
- *Data quality:* MODERATE.

**15. Great Zimbabwe → Mutapa & Torwa successor states (c. 1220–1450/1900; southern Africa, Zimbabwe plateau)**
- *Stress type:* Economic decline (gold-trade shift) → abandonment and self-organizing fragmentation into successor states.
- *MI findings probed:* Deceleration-container (confirms — gold-output/trade decline as decelerator); fragment count (confirms self-organizing 2–4 range — a north–south split into Mutapa and Torwa/Butua).
- *Structural sketch:* A dry-stone-walled (dzimbahwe) elite polity whose wealth rested on cattle, gold mining, and the Sofala/Swahili-coast trade; P4 trade-dependent, P1 inferred from monumental architecture and settlement hierarchy (largely archaeological). As gold trade shifted north in the 15th c. — plus overgrazing, deforestation, drought — the center declined; elites migrated, founding Mutapa (north) and Torwa at Khami (southwest). Mutapa "improved upon Zimbabwe's administrative structure" per the successor record (Portuguese documents + oral tradition). Evidence is largely archaeological with later Portuguese textual overlay.
- *Sources:* Innocent Pikirayi, *The Zimbabwe Culture: Origins and Decline of Southern Zambezian States* (2001); Thomas Huffman, *Snakes and Crocodiles* / *Palaces of Stone*; D.N. Beach, *The Shona and Zimbabwe*.
- *Data quality:* MODERATE.

**16. Kingdom of Aksum — coinage-state rise and trade-isolation decline (c. 100–940 CE; Ethiopia/Eritrea)**
- *Stress type:* Peaceful long decline of a trade-dependent state under exogenous trade-route loss.
- *MI findings probed:* Deceleration-container (confirms — loss of Red Sea/Mediterranean trade as the decelerator); P4–P1 gap (extends — a monetized trade economy whose institutional depth contracted with its revenue).
- *Structural sketch:* The first sub-Saharan state to mint coinage (gold/silver/bronze) and, under Ezana (c. 4th c.), an early official Christian state with the Ge'ez script and monumental stelae. P4 high (Adulis port, ivory/gold tariffs, Rome-to-India trade); P1 moderate. The 7th-c. Persian then Arab control of the Red Sea severed the trade base; coinage ceased by the mid-8th c.; the population retreated to the highlands and the capital was abandoned; the polity faded by c. 960 (the Gudit tradition is sparsely evidenced). Climate/soil-erosion stress is also argued.
- *Sources:* Stuart Munro-Hay, *Aksum: An African Civilisation of Late Antiquity*; David Phillipson, *Foundations of an African Civilisation*; Taddesse Tamrat (post-Aksumite economy).
- *Data quality:* MODERATE.

### F. American (Mesoamerican and Andean) cases

**17. Aztec Triple Alliance — hegemonic tribute empire (1428–1521; central Mexico)**
- *Stress type:* Rapid hegemonic consolidation then conquest-from-outside (Spanish + indigenous allies).
- *MI findings probed:* Suppression typology (confirms strongly — indirect "hegemonic" rule via terror/tribute rather than institutional accommodation; conquered cities defected to Cortés along exactly those fault lines); P4–P1 gap (confirms — large tributary economy on thin integrative institutions).
- *Structural sketch:* A "hegemonic"/indirect empire of three Nahua city-states (Tenochtitlan dominant) leaving local rulers in place against semi-annual tribute and military levies; no garrisons in most provinces; tribute and ritualized terror (mass sacrifice from the 1470s–80s) substituted for integration. P4 high, P1 thin and coercive. When the Spanish arrived (1519), resentful tributaries (e.g., Tlaxcala, Totonacs) defected — the suppressed fault lines became the lines of dissolution. Caveat: the "Triple Alliance" as a formal tripartite institution is partly a post-conquest reconstruction (Gibson).
- *Sources:* Ross Hassig, *Aztec Warfare* and *Mexico and the Spanish Conquest*; Frances Berdan, *The Aztecs of Central Mexico*; Pedro Carrasco, *The Tenochca Empire*.
- *Data quality:* RICH.

**18. Classic Maya → Terminal Classic collapse (c. 250–900 CE; southern lowlands, Mesoamerica)**
- *Stress type:* Self-organizing fragmentation/depopulation of a multi-polity system under drought + endemic warfare.
- *MI findings probed:* Configuration thesis (confirms — a competitive system of city-states with no integrating hegemon, wide vulnerability); deceleration-container (confirms — drought as decelerator on already war-stressed containers); environmental moderator (extends).
- *Structural sketch:* Never a single empire but dozens of competing divine-kingship (k'uhul ajaw) city-states (Tikal, Calakmul, Copán) — P1 fragmented across polities, P4 intensive-agriculture + prestige-goods exchange, P5 chronically war-stressed. A series of severe 9th-c. droughts (paleoclimate records) plus escalating warfare and dynastic delegitimation produced southern-lowland depopulation and the cessation of monument-building, while northern centers (Chichén Itzá) and the highlands persisted. A premier test of the environmental-shock axis.
- *Sources:* Arthur Demarest, *Ancient Maya*; David Webster, *The Fall of the Ancient Maya*; Richardson Gill, *The Great Maya Droughts*.
- *Data quality:* RICH.

**19. Tiwanaku — Andean state collapse (c. 500–1100 CE; Lake Titicaca basin, Bolivia/Peru)**
- *Stress type:* Violent/rapid dissolution of a hierarchical highland state; raised-field agricultural abandonment.
- *MI findings probed:* Deceleration-container (confirms — agricultural-system decline, with a chronology debate over drought timing); suppression-vs-porosity (extends — ideological/ritual integration unraveling fast).
- *Structural sketch:* A powerful, hierarchical highland state integrating colonies (e.g., Moquegua) via ideology, raised-field agriculture, and ceremonial centrality (P1 moderate, ideology-dependent; P4 raised-field intensive agriculture). New Bayesian dating (Marsh et al., PLOS ONE 2023) found "a rapid, city-wide collapse at ~AD 1010–1050, lasting just ~20 years (0–70 years, 95%)... collapse took place well before reduced precipitation, hence this was not a drought-induced societal change," drawn from "Bayesian models of 102 radiocarbon dates, including 45 unpublished dates." This challenges simple climate determinism and points to a cascading breakdown of community/ritual networks once the ceremonial center lost authority.
- *Sources:* Alan Kolata, *The Tiwanaku*; John Janusek, *Ancient Tiwanaku*; Marsh et al., "The center cannot hold: A Bayesian chronology for the collapse of Tiwanaku" (2023).
- *Data quality:* MODERATE.

**20. Wari — Andean imperial experiment (c. 600–1000 CE; central Andes, Peru)**
- *Stress type:* Decline of an expansive but loosely centralized polity; over-centralization as a possible trigger.
- *MI findings probed:* Suppression typology / convergence (extends — centralizing efforts in the 9th c. may have triggered decline, inverting the "institutions rising = developmental" expectation); configuration thesis (confirms — heterogeneous, low-power expansion).
- *Structural sketch:* Often called the first Andean empire, but recent synthesis (Jennings and others) reframes it as a heterogeneous cultural horizon of elite kin-groups competing for followers rather than a tightly administered state — P1 low-to-moderate and contested, P4 administrative-center + colony agriculture. The argument that 9th-c. attempts to centralize decision-making precipitated decline is a useful CHALLENGE to the assumption that rising institutional centralization is always developmental.
- *Sources:* Justin Jennings (ed.), *Beyond Wari Walls*; Luis Lumbreras; Katharina Schreiber, *Wari Imperialism in Middle Horizon Peru*.
- *Data quality:* MODERATE.

### G. European (post-Roman and medieval) cases

**21. Carolingian Empire → Treaty of Verdun partition (800–843/888; western/central Europe)**
- *Stress type:* Engineered partition (partible inheritance) → fragmentation; succession crisis.
- *MI findings probed:* Fragment count (confirms — but tests the typology: a three-way TREATY partition sits between self-organizing 2–4 and engineered-many); suppression-vs-porosity (extends — a thin administrative container reliant on personal/charismatic and ecclesiastical bonds).
- *Structural sketch:* Charlemagne's empire rested on counts, missi dominici, and Church administration over a largely non-monetized agrarian economy — P1 thin relative to territorial extent, P4 low-commercialization agrarian, P5 dependent on the ruler's person. Frankish partible inheritance, not institutional rupture, drove the 843 three-way split (West/Middle/East Francia) among Louis the Pious's sons after the Fontenoy civil war. The unviable Middle Francia corridor then fragmented further — an "engineered" fragmentation along dynastic rather than ethnic/economic fault lines, yielding the seeds of France and Germany.
- *Sources:* Chris Wickham, *The Inheritance of Rome*; Rosamond McKitterick, *The Frankish Kingdoms under the Carolingians*; Janet Nelson, *Charles the Bald*.
- *Data quality:* RICH.

**22. Byzantine Empire — Macedonian revival (c. 867–1056 CE; eastern Mediterranean)**
- *Stress type:* Institutional and territorial revival ("golden age") from a low post-Dark-Age base; positive case.
- *MI findings probed:* "Golden ages are level-and-era, not slope" (confirms strongly — recovery from the 7th–8th-c. "Byzantine Dark Ages" low base, catching the wave of restored security, rising literacy, and the theme-system military-administrative base); P1-ordinality (confirms — high institutional quality underwrote a durable resurgence).
- *Structural sketch:* The theme (military-province) system, a literate urban bureaucracy for tax collection, legal revival (the Basilika), and the Macedonian Renaissance in arts/letters; territorial reconquest (Crete, Cyprus, much of Syria, Bulgaria). P1 high and rising, P3 rising (literacy), P4 expanding (population and production up, provinces secure). The later 11th-c. instability (theme-system noble competition; Manzikert 1071) is the slope-reversal caveat — but the 9th–10th-c. ascent is a clean pre-modern "low-base-catches-exogenous-wave" golden age.
- *Sources:* Mark Whittow, *The Making of Orthodox Byzantium*; Warren Treadgold, *A History of the Byzantine State and Society*; Leonora Neville on Byzantine administration.
- *Data quality:* RICH.

**23. Venetian Republic — millennial institutional durability (c. 1100–1797; northern Adriatic)**
- *Stress type:* Exceptional durability of a constrained-republican constitution; a slow shift from inclusive to extractive institutions.
- *MI findings probed:* P1-ordinality and configuration (confirms — constrained executive + checks/balances → multi-century republican durability); convergence qualifier (extends/CHALLENGES — institutions that "closed" (Serrata) preserved political stability while ossifying economic dynamism, decoupling P1 durability from P4 vitality).
- *Structural sketch:* A constrained doge (binding promissione oath; end of de facto hereditary dogeship by 1032), the Great Council (1172), the Senate, and the Council of Ten (1310) for swift crisis action — a self-consciously "mixed constitution." Early inclusive economic institutions (the commenda/colleganza enabled mobility into long-distance trade) gave way to the Serrata of 1297 (hereditary closure of the Great Council, formalized in the 1315 Libro d'Oro, complete by 1323) and subsequent trade restrictions. Per Acemoglu & Robinson and Puga & Trefler, this elite closure stabilized politics but ended the openness that had powered growth — durable P1, declining P4 dynamism.
- *Sources:* Frederic C. Lane, *Venice: A Maritime Republic* (1973); Puga & Trefler, "International Trade and Institutional Change: Medieval Venice's Response to Globalization," *QJE* 129:2 (2014); Acemoglu & Robinson, *Why Nations Fail* (2012), Venice section.
- *Data quality:* RICH.

### H. Consolidation / early-modern cases

**24. Ottoman Empire — early consolidation (c. 1300–1566; Anatolia, Balkans, eastern Mediterranean)**
- *Stress type:* Successful consolidation and institutional innovation; ascent period.
- *MI findings probed:* Innovation-with-institutions (confirms — devshirme/Janissary and timar systems as institutional innovation underwriting expansion); convergence qualifier (confirms — institutions RISING during territorial expansion = developmental, the "safe" gap); deceleration-container (extends — the later timar→tax-farming shift previews container erosion).
- *Structural sketch:* The devshirme levy created a meritocratic slave-administrative and Janissary military elite loyal to the sultan; the timar system tied cavalry (sipahi) to land-revenue grants; the millet system accommodated religious communities (porosity). P1 high and rising through the 15th–16th c., P4 expanding via conquest and trade-route control, P5 high during expansion. The later (post-1580s) shift from timar to tax-farming (iltizam/malikane) and the rise of provincial ayan notables marks the beginning of container erosion — a clean before/after on the convergence qualifier.
- *Sources:* Halil İnalcık, *The Ottoman Empire: The Classical Age 1300–1600*; Şevket Pamuk on Ottoman factor markets; Colin Imber, *The Ottoman Empire 1300–1650*.
- *Data quality:* RICH.

**25. New Kingdom Egypt → Bronze Age collapse pressures (c. 1550–1077 BCE; Egypt, Levantine empire)**
- *Stress type:* Imperial apex (territorial-tribute empire) then long decline amid systemic Late-Bronze-Age stresses; relatively contained contraction into the Third Intermediate Period.
- *MI findings probed:* Deceleration-container (confirms — Sea Peoples/systemic shocks meeting a priesthood-eroded container); suppression-vs-porosity (extends — Amun priesthood as an internal fault line); environmental/systemic-shock moderator (extends).
- *Structural sketch:* A centralized pharaonic state with professional army, imperial administration over Nubia and the Levant, and tribute inflows (P1 moderate-high, P4 tribute+agrarian). Across the 19th–20th Dynasties, the growing economic/political power of the Amun priesthood at Thebes, fiscal strain (the first recorded labor strike under Ramesses III), Sea Peoples invasions, and broader Late Bronze Age systemic collapse eroded central authority; Egypt fragmented into the Third Intermediate Period (high priests of Amun at Thebes vs. Tanite kings in the north) — a 2-way self-organizing split. Distinct from case #1 in stressor (systemic-international vs. internal-climatic).
- *Sources:* Eric Cline, *1177 B.C.: The Year Civilization Collapsed*; Toby Wilkinson, *The Rise and Fall of Ancient Egypt*; Marc Van de Mieroop, *A History of Ancient Egypt*.
- *Data quality:* RICH.

## Which MI findings have strongest vs. weakest ancient support

**Strongest support:**
1. **Suppression typology** — the clearest cross-cultural regularity. Aztec, Vijayanagara, Inca (existing), Mongol (existing), and Sasanian all decentralized/dissolved along the precise fault lines that force had papered over. This finding looks close to universal in pre-modern data.
2. **Porosity-vs-suppression durability** — the contrast between accommodation-through-channels (Song, Byzantine, Joseon, Ottoman millet, Achaemenid tolerance) and coercive substitution is robust and directionally consistent.
3. **Deceleration-container** — extremely well-attested (8 new cases). Whenever growth decelerated, the outcome tracked whether the institutional/ecological container was strengthening (Tokugawa absorbed; Ottoman classical age) or eroding (Old Kingdom, Khmer, Mali, Aksum ruptured/contracted).
4. **P4–P1 gap with falling institutions = danger** — strong (Maurya, Gupta, Srivijaya, Mali, Aztec).

**Weakest / most challenged support:**
1. **P1-ordinality** — pre-modern shocks (plague, climate, external conquest) repeatedly overwhelmed high-P1 buffers. Sasanian Persia (very high P1, collapse in under two decades) is the sharpest counter; Achaemenid Persia and Song fell to external conquest despite high P1. The finding needs conditioning on shock exogeneity and time horizon.
2. **Fragment count (2–4 self-organizing vs. many engineered)** — partially supported (Han, Old Kingdom, Great Zimbabwe fit the 2–4 self-organizing range), but the Carolingian three-way TREATY partition and Vijayanagara's many nayaka successors blur the self-organizing/engineered boundary; the mechanism (inheritance law, military structure) matters more than the count.
3. **Golden-age "level-and-era, not slope"** — supported in substance (Song, Byzantine, Gupta rose from low/recovering bases) but the "exogenous wave" pre-modern polities caught was demographic/agronomic/printing, not market-integration — a different driver than the modern framework specifies.

## Which findings might need modification for pre-modern contexts

1. **Add an environmental/ecological-container moderator.** In Khmer, Old Kingdom, Classic Maya, Tiwanaku, Aksum, and New Kingdom Egypt, climate/hydraulic/trade-route shocks drove "P5" outcomes substantially independently of institutional quality. The modern MI, built for a post-Malthusian world, likely under-weights exogenous ecological shocks that could overwhelm any pre-modern institutional container.
2. **Re-specify P4 for non-monetized and trade-chokepoint economies.** Srivijaya, Aksum, Mali, and Great Zimbabwe had "economic position" concentrated in control of trade nodes or tribute, not diversified production. The P4–P1 gap finding holds, but P4 must be measured as revenue-base FRAGILITY (single-chokepoint vs. diversified) rather than scale alone.
3. **Condition P1-ordinality on shock type and horizon.** "Higher P1 → better post-stress trajectory" should be restated as "higher P1 → better trajectory CONDITIONAL on the shock being endogenous/economic rather than exogenous-military or pandemic." Pre-modern external-conquest and plague shocks defeat the ordinality.
4. **Distinguish "institutional closure" from "institutional quality."** Venice shows institutions can be simultaneously durable (high P1 stability) and extractive/ossifying (declining P4 dynamism) — the convergence qualifier should separate stability-preserving closure from growth-preserving openness.

## Patterns the modern framework doesn't capture

- **Personalization risk.** Several pre-modern apexes (Maurya post-Ashoka, Mali post-Suleyman, Majapahit post-Gajah Mada/Hayam Wuruk) collapsed because high performance was lodged in individuals, not institutions. The MI's P1 proxy should flag ruler-dependence vs. institutional autonomy — a "bus-factor" dimension largely irrelevant to modern states but decisive pre-modern.
- **Ritual/ideological legitimacy as a load-bearing institution.** Khmer devaraja, Tiwanaku ceremonial centrality, Maya divine kingship, and Egyptian pharaonic flood-legitimacy were genuine institutional load-bearers whose failure cascaded — a category with no clean modern P1 analogue.
- **Adaptive relocation as a non-collapse outcome.** Angkor and Aksum "declined" by relocating their economic centers of gravity rather than rupturing — the binary absorbed/rupture container model may need a third "migrate/transform" outcome for pre-modern polities.
- **Confederacy fragility.** The Sasanian (Parthian-house defection) and Aztec (tributary defection) cases show that nominally centralized states that are actually federations of semi-autonomous elites collapse with startling speed once central coercion lapses — distinct from genuine institutional decentralization.

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt all 25 as a tiered set.** Score the 16 RICH cases first (full five-pillar qualitative assessment), use the 7 MODERATE cases as robustness checks, and treat the most archaeology-dependent cases (Tiwanaku, Wari, Great Zimbabwe; plus Srivijaya/Majapahit on contested texts) as directional only. Benchmark to revisit: if a MODERATE case's pillar scores hinge on a single contested source (e.g., Nagarakretagama tribute claims), down-weight it.
2. **Prioritize the four "challenge" cases for framework stress-testing:** Sasanian Persia (P1-ordinality), Venice (convergence/closure), Wari (centralization-as-trigger), and Joseon (lower-maximal-P1 longevity). If the MI's findings survive these, confidence in cross-era universality rises materially.
3. **Build the deceleration-container test on the 8-case sub-panel** (Old Kingdom, Khmer, Tang, Mali, Aksum, New Kingdom, Tiwanaku, Maya). This is where pre-modern evidence is densest and the absorbed-vs-rupture mechanism most cleanly observable. Threshold that would change the recommendation: if container-state (strengthening/eroding) fails to predict outcome in ≥3 of 8, the finding does not travel pre-modern.
4. **Commission targeted primary-source scoring for the two thinnest pillars in archaeological cases — P2 (innovation) and P3 (human capital).** For Tiwanaku, Wari, and Great Zimbabwe these are nearly unobservable; either accept P1/P4/P5-only scoring or drop them from quantitative work.
5. **Add a pre-modern moderator layer** (environmental shock; revenue-base fragility; ruler-dependence; confederacy-vs-unitary) before any cross-era regression. Without it, the high-P1 collapse cases will look like noise rather than the conditioning information they actually are.
6. **Seek a Pacific/Oceanic case** (e.g., precontact Hawaiian chiefdoms or Rapa Nui) in a later round to close the one empty geographic cell — but only if evidence clears the qualitative-five-pillar bar; on current evidence neither reliably does.

## Caveats

- **Evidence asymmetry is severe.** Textually rich literate empires (China, Rome, Persia, the Islamic world) support far more confident scoring than archaeology-dependent cases (Tiwanaku, Wari, Great Zimbabwe). There is a real risk of a "literacy bias" in which the MI looks more universal simply because well-documented states resemble each other.
- **Several causal stories remain contested.** Angkor (climate vs. religious-economic shift), Tiwanaku (drought timing vs. social cascade — recent Bayesian dating puts collapse BEFORE the drought), Old Kingdom (Nile-flood vs. political), and the Aztec Triple Alliance (whether it was a formal tripartite institution at all) are live scholarly debates; I have flagged these in-case rather than picking a winner.
- **Do not over-fit modern pillar definitions.** "Innovation" (P2) and "economic structure" (P4) in particular risk anachronism — pre-modern "innovation" was often agronomic/hydraulic/administrative, not technological-commercial, and "economic structure" was often tribute/chokepoint rather than production-diversified.
- **The Ottoman, Venice, and Tokugawa cases straddle the spirit of the 1800 CE boundary** (early-modern institutional forms) but all have their tested apex/transition within the pre-modern window.
- **Two consolidation decisions on the suggested list:** I folded Mutapa into the Great Zimbabwe case (same cultural-institutional lineage, avoids double-counting) and treated Edo/Tokugawa as one case (Edo period = Tokugawa period). I substituted Classic Maya and Wari to strengthen the environmental-shock and centralization-as-trigger tests, which otherwise had no representation.
