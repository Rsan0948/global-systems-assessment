# Case Study Selection: Revisions, Replacements, and Bonus Cases

**Date:** June 27, 2026
**Purpose:** Address two overlap concerns in the proposed 30-case expansion, propose African replacements that fill additional coverage gaps, and retain the original cases as bonus candidates for future rounds.

---

## CONCERN 1: Case 49 — Singapore (Standalone Consolidation)

### The Problem

Singapore already appears as half of Case 7 (Singapore/Malaysia split, 1965). Case 49 proposes scoring Singapore again as a standalone consolidation case on a different time window and stress type.

The brief argues this is non-duplicative because Case 7 tests the fragmentation event while Case 49 tests decades-later consolidation outcomes. This is technically defensible — they are different questions about the same entity. However, a reviewer encountering "Singapore" twice in a 50-case baseline will reasonably ask whether one of those slots would generate more independent information as a genuinely new entity.

The deeper issue: Singapore's MI profile is already the framework's best-documented positive case. It was the cleanest confirmation in Round 2, it anchors the complexity-control thesis, and its pillar scores are cited extensively throughout the analysis. Adding a second Singapore case produces diminishing marginal information — the framework already knows what Singapore looks like and has already been confirmed by it.

The effectiveness/voice split that Case 49 is designed to test (high capacity, constrained pluralism) is better tested by an entity the framework has NOT already confirmed on, because a new entity provides genuinely independent evidence while a second Singapore scoring is partially correlated with the first.

### The Replacement: Case 49 → Mauritius

**Mauritius** is the replacement that maximizes independent information gain while testing the same structural questions Case 49 was designed to test — plus additional questions Singapore cannot test.

1. **Entities:** Mauritius
2. **Region:** East Africa / Indian Ocean (island nation)
3. **Stress type:** Successful consolidation / post-colonial democratic success
4. **Time period:** 1968 independence → 2024; score ~1996 vs 2023
5. **Gap filled:** Additional sub-Saharan Africa; island-nation complexity-control test; successful consolidation (positive case); the small-island geographic advantage thesis; the only African island-state democracy in the dataset
6. **What it tests:**
   - The complexity-control thesis for island nations — but with a critical twist. Unlike Singapore (which controlled complexity through demographic engineering and restrictive immigration), Mauritius is genuinely multi-ethnic (Hindu ~48%, Creole ~27%, Muslim ~17%, Chinese ~3%) and genuinely democratic (Freedom House "Free" continuously since independence with one brief exception). This tests whether the island geographic advantage alone enables stability even WITHOUT Singapore-style demographic control.
   - P1 ordinality among positive cases — Mauritius's P1 should predict its superior trajectory relative to mainland African states with comparable post-colonial starting points.
   - Whether the framework explains African success as well as African failure. The current African cases are heavily weighted toward failure and conflict (Sudan, Nigeria/Biafra, Ethiopia, DRC). Mauritius and Botswana (Case 38) together provide the positive counterweight.
   - The earned-vs-granted prosperity distinction — Mauritius has minimal natural resources (no oil, no diamonds, limited land) and built prosperity through textiles, tourism, financial services, and institutional quality. It's the African Singapore but achieved through democracy rather than authoritarianism, making it a genuinely different test.
7. **Why Mauritius over other alternatives:**
   - Tests the same effectiveness/voice question as Singapore #49 but from the OPPOSITE direction — Mauritius has high voice AND high effectiveness, while Singapore has high effectiveness with constrained voice. Together with Rwanda (high effectiveness, low voice) and Singapore (Case 7), the framework now has three configurations of the effectiveness/voice relationship to compare.
   - Provides a genuinely independent data point — the framework has never scored Mauritius, so the confirmation or falsification is fully independent.
   - Fills the "African democratic success" gap that Botswana alone doesn't fully cover (Botswana's success is partly resource-driven; Mauritius's is purely institutional).
   - Fills the "Indian Ocean island nation" geographic gap.
8. **Data availability:** GOOD. Mauritius has full WGI coverage from 1996, full HDI coverage, World Bank WDI data. One of the best-measured African states.
9. **Difficulty:** MODERATE TEST. The framework should predict success based on P1, but the test is whether it correctly identifies WHY Mauritius succeeds — institutional quality plus island geographic advantage plus genuine democratic accountability — and whether it can distinguish Mauritius's success mechanism from Singapore's different mechanism.
10. **Preliminary hypothesis:** High P1 with democratic accountability (unlike Singapore's constrained voice) predicts durable stability and prosperity. The island geographic advantage reduces complexity-management demands. Mauritius validates Strategy 1 (porosity/democracy) at small island scale, while Singapore validates Strategy 3 (complexity control) at small island scale — two different strategies, both successful, both explained by the framework through different mechanisms.

**HDI context:** Mauritius HDI 0.796 (2023), classified "High Human Development" — the highest in sub-Saharan Africa. GDP per capita PPP ~$26,000. Gini coefficient ~36.8. Consistently ranked among Africa's least corrupt and most democratic states.

## CONCERN 2: Case 47 — Ethiopia Federal System (Standalone Design Case)

### The Problem

Ethiopia already appears in Case 8 (Ethiopia/Eritrea, 1993 fragmentation), Case 11 (Ethiopia/Tigray, 2020-2022 war), and arguably Case 24/25 (DRC/Rwanda comparative which involves Ethiopia's regional role). Case 47 proposes scoring Ethiopia again as a standalone ethnic-federalism design case.

The brief distinguishes this from Case 11 — one is about the war event, the other is about the constitutional design. This is a valid analytical distinction. However, functionally the case scores the same country at overlapping time points using the same WGI data. The result on Case 47 is not statistically independent of Case 11's result because the underlying entity's MI profile is identical — the same P1, same P5, same configuration.

The ethnic-federalism design question is important and worth testing. But testing it on an entity already scored three times in the baseline produces correlated evidence rather than independent evidence. The marginal information gain from a fourth Ethiopia case is lower than a first case from a new entity testing the same structural question.

### The Replacement: Case 47 → Cameroon (Anglophone Crisis)

**Cameroon's Anglophone crisis** fills the ethnic-federalism design gap while providing genuinely independent evidence from a new entity — and simultaneously fills the Francophone Africa gap the coverage map identified as remaining after 50 cases.

1. **Entities:** Cameroon (central state vs. Anglophone Northwest and Southwest regions)
2. **Region:** Central/West Africa (bridges both sub-regions)
3. **Stress type:** Suppressed fragmentation / colonial-engineering failure / institutional design
4. **Time period:** 2016 Anglophone protests → 2017 declaration of "Ambazonia" → ongoing armed conflict; score ~2015 vs 2023
5. **Gap filled:**
   - Francophone Africa (the coverage map noted this as still thin after 50 cases)
   - Colonial engineering as a direct cause of fragmentation (the Anglophone regions were British-mandate territory merged with French-mandate Cameroon at independence in 1961 — a textbook engineered border combining two different colonial-institutional legacies)
   - An active, ongoing suppressed secession distinct from Myanmar (different region, different institutional context, different colonial legacy)
   - Safeguard G Tier 1 (military suppression) in a Francophone African context
6. **What it tests:**
   - The colonial-engineering thesis at its most precise: two populations with DIFFERENT colonial institutional legacies (British common law vs French civil law, different administrative traditions, different educational systems, different languages) forced into one state at independence. The MI should predict that this specific configuration — two institutional traditions compressed into one state — produces fragmentation pressure along the institutional fault line. And that's exactly where the separatist movement formed.
   - Safeguard G Tier 1: Cameroon's response has been military suppression of the Anglophone regions. The framework predicts this delays but does not resolve the complexity-capacity mismatch, and predicts continued instability. The ongoing conflict (2017-present with thousands killed and over 700,000 displaced) confirms directionally, but the case tests whether the framework can predict the FORM of the instability (separatist insurgency along the colonial-institutional fault line rather than ethnic conflict or economic crisis).
   - Whether low-moderate P1 with high internal institutional heterogeneity (two different legal systems operating within one state) produces predictable fragmentation pressure. This is a more precise test than Nigeria/Biafra because the heterogeneity is specifically INSTITUTIONAL (different legal and administrative traditions) rather than primarily ethnic or religious.
   - Safeguard D: Cameroon's Anglophone regions border Nigeria, which has its own Anglophone identity. The cross-border dimension adds an exogenous factor the framework should flag.
7. **Why Cameroon over other alternatives:**
   - Directly tests colonial-institutional engineering as a fragmentation driver — the single most important gap the existing case studies don't fully isolate. Nigeria tests post-colonial engineering but with 250+ ethnic groups the signal is noisy. Cameroon's Anglophone crisis is almost a controlled experiment: two populations with different institutional inheritances merged into one state, with the fragmentation occurring exactly along the institutional boundary.
   - Fills the Francophone Africa gap that the coverage map explicitly identified.
   - Provides a genuinely independent data point — the framework has never scored Cameroon.
   - Provides an ongoing/active case that can be tracked prospectively alongside Myanmar, Sudan, and Bougainville.
   - Tests whether the framework can handle institutional heterogeneity WITHIN a state as a complexity variable — a question the existing cases mostly test through ethnic, religious, or geographic heterogeneity rather than institutional-legacy heterogeneity.
8. **Data availability:** GOOD. Cameroon has full WGI coverage from 1996, HDI coverage, World Bank WDI data. The Anglophone regions don't have separate WGI scores (sub-national limitation), but the colonial-institutional analysis is well-documented in the academic literature.
9. **Difficulty:** MODERATE TEST. The framework should predict instability along the institutional fault line given low-moderate P1, but the specific prediction about institutional-legacy heterogeneity as a complexity driver is a genuine extension of the framework's logic that hasn't been tested before.
10. **Preliminary hypothesis:** Low-moderate P1 with dual colonial-institutional legacies predicts fragmentation pressure along the institutional boundary. Military suppression (Safeguard G Tier 1) predicts ongoing instability without resolution. The framework should correctly identify that the Anglophone crisis is driven by institutional-legacy mismatch (the complexity-capacity problem is specifically that the Francophone institutional framework cannot govern populations socialized into a different institutional tradition) rather than by ethnic hatred or economic grievance, although those compound the pressure.

**Context:** The International Crisis Group has extensively documented the crisis. OCHA reports over 4.7 million people in need in the Northwest and Southwest regions. The government has oscillated between military operations and limited dialogue offers. The separatist movement remains fragmented but persistent, consistent with the Safeguard G Tier 1 prediction of unresolved pressure.

## ADDITIONAL AFRICAN CASE (filling the expanded gap)

The two replacements above add Mauritius and Cameroon to the African coverage. Given the importance of sub-Saharan Africa to the framework's claims — and the fact that Africa contains more post-colonial engineered states than any other continent — one more African case strengthens the baseline significantly.

### Proposed Addition: Case 51 (Bonus Promoted to Core) — Somaliland / Somalia

This case is unique in the entire 50-case baseline because it tests something no other case does: a DE FACTO state that functions but is internationally unrecognized, alongside a formally recognized state that has collapsed.

1. **Entities:** Somalia (recognized, collapsed); Somaliland (unrecognized, functional)
2. **Region:** East Africa / Horn of Africa
3. **Stress type:** De facto fragmentation with inverted recognition — the functional entity is unrecognized while the failed entity retains full sovereignty
4. **Time period:** 1991 Somaliland declaration of independence → 2024; score ~1996 vs 2023
5. **Gap filled:**
   - The "unrecognized functional state" phenomenon — the framework predicts outcomes based on institutional quality, but Somaliland's international non-recognition means its institutional success has not been rewarded with statehood. Does the MI's logic apply equally to entities regardless of recognition status?
   - Additional Horn of Africa coverage
   - A direct test of whether formal sovereignty or actual institutional capacity determines outcomes (the framework claims capacity; the international system claims sovereignty)
   - Tests complexity-capacity matching from the BOTTOM — Somaliland deliberately reduced its complexity (smaller territory, more homogeneous population than all-Somalia) to match its limited institutional capacity, which is the Strategy 3 logic applied to a fragmentation context
6. **What it tests:**
   - P1 ordinality in the most extreme recognition-inverted case on earth. If the MI's P1 logic is correct, Somaliland (higher functional P1) should outperform Somalia (lower P1) on every outcome measure regardless of recognition status. And it does — peaceful elections, functioning courts, lower violence, growing economy. But the framework has to grapple with the fact that institutional success without recognition produces a ceiling effect the MI doesn't model.
   - The complexity-control thesis applied to fragmentation — Somaliland's relative success compared to Somalia is partly because it carved out a LESS complex entity (more clan homogeneity, smaller territory, pre-existing British-colonial institutional legacy distinct from Italian-colonial Somalia). This is the Strategy 3 logic: reduce complexity to match available capacity.
   - Safeguard I in reverse — normally the backstop patron enables RE-suppression. Here, the ABSENCE of a backstop (no great power championing Somaliland's independence) prevents recognition despite functional statehood. Does the framework explain why Somaliland hasn't achieved recognition despite institutional superiority?
7. **Data availability:** MODERATE for Somaliland (no separate WGI — scored within Somalia or not scored at all); MODERATE for Somalia (WGI coverage exists but the scores are essentially measuring Mogadishu and little else). Sub-national assessment required.
8. **Difficulty:** HARD STRESS TEST. This case could reveal that the MI's assumption that institutional quality translates into outcomes regardless of formal recognition is incomplete — Somaliland's ceiling is imposed by the international system, not by its institutional capacity.
9. **Preliminary hypothesis:** Somaliland's higher functional P1 predicts superior stability, governance delivery, and human development relative to Somalia — confirmed by available evidence. But the framework must explain why functional P1 superiority has not produced recognition, which requires acknowledging that the international system's sovereignty norms are an external constraint the MI doesn't capture.

## BONUS CASES (retained for future rounds)

The following cases were either replaced from the core 50 or considered during selection. They remain strong candidates for future expansion beyond 50, particularly if the framework develops the capacity to handle their specific structural questions better after the 50-case baseline is established.

### Bonus A: Singapore Standalone Consolidation (original Case 49)

**Why retained as bonus:** Singapore's long-term consolidation under one-party dominance remains an important test of whether the framework over-weights voice relative to effectiveness. However, this test is now partially covered by the Rwanda/Vietnam/Mauritius trio in the main baseline, which provides three different configurations of the effectiveness/voice relationship.

**When to run:** After the 50-case baseline is established, if the effectiveness/voice decomposition question remains unresolved. Particularly valuable if P1 IS decomposed — a standalone Singapore case would then score differently than Case 7 and provide genuinely new information.

### Bonus B: Ethiopia Federal System (original Case 47)

**Why retained as bonus:** The ethnic-federalism design question is genuinely important and Ethiopia is the most consequential living test of it. However, the overlapping data with Cases 8, 11, and 24/25 means this case provides correlated rather than independent evidence in the current configuration.

**When to run:** After the 50-case baseline is established, as part of a dedicated "institutional design" module that also includes Bosnia's post-Dayton federalism, Lebanon's confessional system, and perhaps Nigeria's 36-state federalism — all scored as design cases rather than event cases. This module would test whether the MI can evaluate institutional DESIGN choices independently of the stress events that occur within those designs.

### Bonus C: Zimbabwe (institutional collapse and attempted reconstruction)

**Why considered:** Zimbabwe under Mugabe is one of the most dramatic institutional-collapse cases in post-colonial Africa — a country that was once "the breadbasket of Africa" with relatively strong institutions (inherited from Rhodesian settler state) that were systematically hollowed out through land reform, patronage, and authoritarian consolidation. The MI should show P1 declining dramatically from relatively high post-independence levels. The post-Mugabe period (2017-present) tests whether institutional restoration is possible after prolonged capture.

**Why not in core 50:** South Africa (Case 13) and Rwanda (Case 25) already test institutional reconstruction in southern/central Africa. Zimbabwe adds a "collapse from a higher starting point" variant but the marginal information gain over South Africa was judged lower than Cameroon's or Mauritius's contribution to filling unstudied gaps.

**When to run:** After the 50-case baseline, particularly if the framework develops a "institutional capture" safeguard beyond the current Safeguard E rentier-capture mechanism. Zimbabwe is the canonical POLITICAL capture case (as opposed to RESOURCE capture), and would test whether a new "patronage capture" safeguard is needed.

### Bonus D: Hong Kong (re-suppression after porosity, 2020 National Security Law)

**Why considered:** Hong Kong is a DIRECT test of the re-suppression-after-porosity finding (Safeguard G / Mod9) in a high-capacity context. Unlike Myanmar (low P1, catastrophic outcome), Hong Kong had very high P1 (among the world's best governance scores) when China imposed the National Security Law in 2020, effectively ending the "one country, two systems" porosity period. The framework predicts re-suppression after porosity produces worse outcomes than continuous suppression — but does that hold when the re-suppressor has extremely high state capacity?

**Why not in core 50:** Data challenges. Hong Kong's WGI coverage is complicated by its status within China. The governance scores may not reflect post-2020 reality yet. And the case is still very recent — the long-term outcome of the National Security Law's impact on Hong Kong's institutional character is not yet clear enough to score definitively.

**When to run:** After the 50-case baseline, ideally in 2028-2030 when the medium-term effects of the National Security Law are measurable in governance data. This is potentially the single most important future case for the re-suppression finding because it tests it at a P1 level (very high) that Myanmar and Sudan (very low) didn't cover.

### Bonus E: Afghanistan (external administration failure)

**Why considered:** Afghanistan is arguably the most important external-administration case of the 21st century — 20 years of US/NATO state-building that collapsed in weeks when the external administrator withdrew (August 2021). It would be a devastating test of Safeguard A and the external-administration thesis generally.

**Why not in core 50:** Iraq (Case 30) already tests external administration in MENA with better data availability. Afghanistan's data is extremely limited, particularly for the Taliban period (2021-present), and the WGI scores during the NATO period are heavily confounded by international presence. The case is valuable but the data constraints make the scoring unreliable enough that it's better as a qualitative illustration than a quantitative case study.

**When to run:** After the 50-case baseline, as part of a dedicated "external administration" module alongside Iraq, Bosnia, Kosovo, and East Timor. The qualitative findings would complement the quantitative limitations.

## STRUCTURAL NOTE: The Rwanda Architectural Decision

Per the analysis, Rwanda should be scored BEFORE any batch as an architectural decision point. The question is whether P1 remains a single composite or must be decomposed into "state capacity" and "institutional accountability."

**Recommended process:**

1. Score Rwanda with P1 as composite (current architecture)
2. Score Rwanda with P1 decomposed into P1a (Government Effectiveness, Regulatory Quality) and P1b (Rule of Law, Voice/Accountability, Control of Corruption)
3. Compare which version produces the more accurate prediction
4. If decomposition is clearly better: implement across the full framework BEFORE running Batches 1-6
5. If composite works: proceed as designed
6. If ambiguous: run Vietnam (Case 31) as a second test of the same question before deciding

The decision should be documented formally and included in the companion guide's methodology section, because it represents the single most consequential architectural choice in the framework's development since the original correlation-derived weight adjustment.
